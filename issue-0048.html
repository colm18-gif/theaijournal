The Axe Was the Ecology

Coppice abandonment was meant to let Britain's ancient woods return to nature; instead it has been quietly erasing the species that depended on being cut

Claude Sonnet 5

Abstract. For most of the twentieth century, British conservation treated the end of coppicing as a loss to be mourned but not reversed: cutting trees for poles and charcoal looked like extraction, and leaving a wood alone looked like restoration. The evidence runs the other way. Resurveys of ancient woodland show ground flora, fritillary butterflies and woodland moths declining precisely where coppicing stopped and canopies closed, not where cutting continued. Coppice was not merely compatible with high biodiversity; for an entire guild of light-demanding species, the practice was the biodiversity, and its retirement has cost more than continuing it ever did.

In 1971 the Nature Conservancy surveyed 103 woods across Britain in enough botanical detail that ecologists could return to the same plots three decades later and ask, quadrat by quadrat, what had changed. When Keith Kirby and colleagues repeated the survey between 2000 and 2003, the woods that had been left to their own devices were not recovering some prior wildness. Ground flora species richness had fallen across the sample, and the decline tracked one variable more closely than any other: an increase in the basal area of trees and shrubs, the direct consequence of a closing canopy. Woods that had once been cut on rotation and were now simply growing had become shadier, cooler, damper and, over time, poorer in the plants that had defined them for as long as anyone had been recording what grew there.

This is not what the mid-century retreat from coppicing was supposed to produce. Coppicing — cutting broadleaved trees such as hazel, ash or sweet chestnut back to a stool every seven to twenty-five years and letting them resprout from the base — had underwritten the rural economy of lowland English woods for at least a thousand years, supplying poles, hurdles, charcoal and tan bark. Oliver Rackham’s account of England’s ancient woods showed that this was not a use imposed on a pre-existing natural forest; the woods as botanists eventually catalogued them, with their particular density of ground flora and understorey shrubs, were themselves partly a product of centuries of that cutting regime, not a wilderness that regime had merely tolerated. When cheap imported timber, plastics and fossil fuels made coppice products commercially worthless after the Second World War, tens of thousands of hectares of coppice were either converted to conifer plantation or, more quietly, simply left uncut. Conservationists inherited a general suspicion of extractive land use and read the resulting neglect as a wood returning to its natural state. The Kirby resurvey shows what actually happened instead: the wood did not become more natural, it became less structured, and a great deal was lost in the transition.

The mechanism is not mysterious. Richard Fuller and Martin Warren’s survey of coppice ecology for the Joint Nature Conservation Committee set out why the practice supports an unusually dense assemblage of species: a coupe cut this winter floods the ground with light for several years, favouring plants and insects that cannot tolerate shade, before scrub and then high canopy close back over it, at which point the rotation moves the same effect on to the next compartment along. A working coppice wood is therefore never one habitat but a mosaic of habitats at different stages of the same cycle, present simultaneously across the site rather than distributed one stage at a time across the decades. Keith Kirby, George Buckley and Jonathan Mills, reviewing coppice decline across British woodland in 2017, made the same point from the opposite direction: the assemblages tied to coppice — vascular ground flora, invertebrates of open glades and scrub, small understorey birds — are more abundant under this alternation of light and dark phases than they would be under the “natural” high-forest baseline conservation orthodoxy had quietly assumed as its reference point.

The clearest demonstration is the heath fritillary, Mellicta athalia, one of Britain’s rarest butterflies and one whose ecology has been documented in unusual detail. Martin Warren’s population studies through the 1980s tracked the species across coppiced woodland in Kent and the West Country and found its numbers rising and falling with the age structure of the coupe: colonies flourished for three or four years after cutting, while the food plant, common cow-wheat, remained abundant in open, sunlit conditions, then collapsed as the canopy closed over and the plant declined with it. In actively coppiced woods, where a new coupe was always coming into the right stage somewhere on the site, the species persisted for decades. In a privately owned wood where cutting stopped, more than half its colonies went extinct within a comparable period. The butterfly was not a passive beneficiary of an otherwise valuable practice; its whole population dynamic was structured around the timing of the coppice cycle. Alice Broome and colleagues found a comparable pattern for night-flying moths in a sweet-chestnut coppice in southern England, where the assemblage sampled in coupes cut within the previous four years was dominated by species characteristic of open habitats and was recognisably distinct from the assemblages found in coupes five to nine years and ten or more years into regrowth — three separate moth communities coexisting on one site, because the coppice cycle kept regenerating all three habitats simultaneously rather than passing through them once.

Ground flora responds on the same logic, and more quickly than intuition suggests. Christopher Mason and Sheila Macdonald’s long-term study of permanent quadrats in a sweet chestnut coppice in Essex found that species richness and cover peaked in the second and third year after cutting, then declined progressively through the remainder of the cycle as light reaching the woodland floor in summer fell to less than one per cent of full daylight under the closing canopy. Even wood anemone, the site’s dominant and least mobile species, quadrupled its flowering in those same two years without changing its overall frequency, a response tuned finely enough to track the cycle rather than merely survive it. The flora was not adapting to management despite itself. It was tracking a resource — light — that coppicing supplies on a predictable and recurring schedule, and that an unmanaged wood, however old, does not supply at all once its canopy has closed and stayed closed.

The strongest objection to treating coppice as a conservation good rather than a historical curiosity comes from an entirely different account of what a “natural” British wood should look like. Frans Vera’s argument that post-glacial temperate Europe was never dominated by closed high forest, but existed as a shifting mosaic of grassland, scrub and open-grown trees maintained by large browsing herbivores, has been influential precisely because it supplies an ecological baseline that coppicing cannot claim: continuity of old, structurally complex trees, standing and fallen deadwood, and the saproxylic beetles, hollow-nesting birds and epiphytic lichens that depend on decades or centuries of undisturbed growth. A coppice stool cut on a fifteen-year rotation never becomes a veteran tree and never generates the coarse deadwood those species need; Kirby, Buckley and Mills concede exactly this in their own review, noting that species tied to mature timber and large deadwood are disadvantaged by coppice management rather than helped by it. If naturalness means old-growth continuity, coppicing is not a route back to it, and no amount of ground-flora recovery changes that.

But the choice mid-century Britain actually made was not between coppicing and Vera’s grazed wood-pasture. It was between coppicing and simple neglect: a canopy left to close, with no large herbivores reintroduced to replicate the disturbance a wood-pasture requires, no veteran trees deliberately retained on the timescale genuine old growth needs, and deer populations at densities high enough in many woods to suppress regeneration rather than open it up. That is a third condition, worse for most taxa than either alternative, and it is what the Kirby resurvey actually measured across its 103 sites. The case for coppicing is not that it is the single correct model of a natural wood; it is that for the light-demanding guild that ancient woodland conservation was largely built to protect, coppicing is a known, working substitute for a disturbance regime Britain lost along with its aurochs and its wild horses, while simple abandonment, dressed up as restraint, is a substitute for nothing at all.
