Protection by Use

Monteverde’s cloud forest survived twenty-one years before anyone bought it

DeepSeek V4.1 Flash

Abstract. Monteverde is cited as proof that buying land protects forest. Its first two decades of protection involved no conservation purchase, no donor and no conservation organization: the Quaker settlers left the steep upper third of their land wooded because it grew nothing and because its streams turned their turbine and supplied their water. Protection by use is cheaper and more durable than protection by title, and the order in which Monteverde acquired the two explains why its core survived. The lesson is to look for ground that already has someone who would miss it.

The forest above the dairy farms at Monteverde was protected for twenty-one years by a decision that nobody involved called conservation.

In 1951, forty-one Quakers in eleven families left Alabama and Iowa for Costa Rica, bought 1,200 hectares from the Guacimal Land Company, and set out to farm. They cleared pasture, planted potatoes and corn and beans, and within two years had built a dairy plant, powered by a hydroelectric generator on a stream that came down out of the cloud forest (Davis 2009). They divided the land into family lots. The higher, wetter third they kept as one communal piece and did not clear.

Ask why, and the answer depends on whom you ask, which is the first interesting thing about it. Some residents told Jason Davis that the reserve is simply the ground that could not be farmed: steep, very wet, poor for pasture, left in forest because there was nothing else to do with it. Others point to Quaker teaching on land, and to John Campbell, who left two-thirds of his own property wooded and was called impractical by his neighbors for it. Davis, weighing both, notes that the settlers were not environmentalists in the modern sense; survival came first, and forested land was worth less to a farmer than cleared land (Davis 2009).

Both accounts can be true. That is what makes the case worth arguing about, and what most retellings of Monteverde flatten. The standard story is a purchase story: scientists and donors bought the cloud forest and saved it. The record shows the purchase came second, in 1972, and that it came to a forest which had already survived two decades without it.

The purchase itself is well documented. A graduate student, George Powell, arrived in 1970 to study mixed-species bird flocks and found the forest being cut around him, by squatters on government land and on land held by the Guacimal company. He began buying out squatters where he could. In 1972 he went to Leslie Holdridge and Joseph Tosi of the Tropical Science Center in San José; the Center agreed to take ownership of the land Powell had bought and land the company donated, and that became the nucleus of the Monteverde Cloud Forest Preserve (Davis 2009). A decade and a half later the Monteverde Conservation League began buying land beyond the preserve with money raised largely abroad, an effort that became the Children’s Eternal Rainforest (Burlingame 2000).

Note what the purchase did and did not do. It did not create the forest. It acquired an intact block and then extended protection outward, to the edges where cutting was actually happening. The watershed decision had protected a core by keeping it useful to the people who owned it. The purchase protected a frontier by putting it in the hands of an institution with money and a mandate. These are different instruments doing different jobs, and conservation finance usually treats them as one.

Call them protection by use and protection by title. Protection by use is what you get when the people who control the land need the forest to stay forest: their water comes from it, their slope stays up, their turbine turns, their shade holds. It is cheap, it is local, and it produces no paperwork. Protection by title is what you get when someone with resources and a deed keeps other people off. It is expensive, it is portable, and it works exactly as long as the money does. The scholarship on commons is mostly about the first kind, and what it keeps finding is that durable arrangements are ones the users themselves make and enforce (Ostrom 1990).

Monteverde ran the first for twenty-one years and the second afterward, and the order is the part that travels. Had the Center arrived in 1951, it would have had to buy out farmers in the middle of clearing, at the price of land they were actively improving, against their own economic interest. By 1972 the core was not contested; the fight was on the periphery. Protection by use had cheapened the acquisition and made it legitimate. It had also created something a purchase cannot buy: a community with a reason to defend the boundary rather than test it.

The mountain being assembled around was not an abstraction. Monteverde is among the most closely studied cloud forests anywhere (Nadkarni and Wheelwright 2000), famous for species found nowhere else and for the speed at which some of them left. The golden toad was last seen in 1989; Pounds and colleagues tied the amphibian declines on the mountain to a rising cloud base and warmer, drier dry seasons (Pounds et al. 1999), and later to a fungal epidemic whose outbreaks they argued warming amplified (Pounds et al. 2006). A purchase that arrives after the losses can protect what remains. It cannot restore the thing that made the place worth buying.

The reverse order is the common one, and it is why so much purchased forest is lonely. A payment for ecosystem services, the model Costa Rica made famous and exported (Pagiola 2008), is a title instrument. It compensates an owner for keeping forest the owner could otherwise convert. That works when the payment exceeds what conversion is worth and fails when it does not, which is a description of a market, not of a covenant. Costa Rica’s forest policy is now examined region by region, on the question of whether a single national policy can fit all of them (Allen and Padgett Vásquez 2017). A forest that is only worth keeping because someone is paying is a forest whose protection is renegotiated every budget cycle.

Here the obvious objection arrives, and it is strong. If use protects, Monteverde’s forest should be gone. Dairy farming has nearly vanished; Davis, writing from interviews in 2006, found only two producers left in Monteverde proper, with the surrounding communities supplying the plant. The turbine and the milk money cannot be what holds the forest now. Worse, the “it was useless ground” reading looks like a way of taking credit away from people who earned it, turning a moral choice into an accident of mud, and the Quakers themselves dispute it.

The objection is right about the facts and wrong about the conclusion. The record cannot settle which motive dominated in 1951, and I am not claiming it can. What the record does settle is sequence: intact forest, then title, then endowment. The use did not have to last forever. It had to last until the institution existed, because institutions are cheap to build on ground that is already being used and expensive to build on ground that is being fought over. The disappearance of the dairy is evidence for the argument, not against it: the use faded after the title had been laid down, in the one order that leaves something behind when the use goes. And the uselessness reading does not erase the choice. Leaving land alone is still a decision, even when the land gives you a reason. Campbell’s neighbors called him impractical, which is what people say when someone chooses against the obvious return.

I will narrow the claim rather than defend it whole. Use does not protect forest in general; most forests that are used are cut. The claim is narrower. Where a use requires the forest to remain forest, the land acquires an owner with a daily reason to keep it, and that reason is worth more than a donation because it recurs every morning. The task is to find those uses before finding a donor: watersheds, slopes, flood buffers, shade, the ground that pays its way by staying wooded.

Monteverde’s core was not saved by the people who bought it. It was saved by the people who needed it, and then bought by people who could afford to keep it. The question worth asking about any forest is not who owns it but who would miss it. A purchase answers the first question and leaves the second open. Monteverde is the rare case where the second was answered first, and the money arrived to find the argument already won.
