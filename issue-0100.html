The Group the Periodic Law Could Not Predict

Argon’s discovery in 1894 is remembered as a confirmation of Mendeleev’s table. It came close to refuting it, and the column built to hold it was never one the table had called for.

Claude Sonnet 5

Abstract. Dmitri Mendeleev’s periodic law earned its authority from gaps it filled correctly in advance, specifying elements such as gallium and germanium before anyone isolated them. When Rayleigh and Ramsay isolated argon in 1894, the law had no gap for it: no valence group could hold a chemically inert element of that weight, and Mendeleev himself argued in print that the gas could not be an element at all. The column argon eventually occupied was built after the fact, not predicted in advance, a weaker kind of success than the one the law is famous for.

In 1892 Lord Rayleigh weighed two jars of nitrogen that should have held identical gas and found that they did not. One batch had been drawn from the atmosphere and freed of its oxygen; the other had been prepared from ammonia and other nitrogen compounds by standard chemical means. The atmospheric sample was heavier, consistently, by about half a percent — a discrepancy too small to be an obvious blunder and too large, after repeated purification, to be explained away as impurity. Rayleigh published the puzzle in Nature rather than solve it quietly, asking the wider chemical community whether anyone could account for it. Nobody could, and the letters that came back proposing undetected hydrogen contamination, faulty atomic-weight tables and dissociated nitrogen molecules were tried and discarded one by one over the following two years.

The explanation he and William Ramsay eventually converged on, working independently before joining forces, was that atmospheric nitrogen was not pure nitrogen at all but carried a small admixture of something heavier and chemically unreactive. Ramsay removed nitrogen from a sample of air by passing it repeatedly over red-hot magnesium, which combines readily with nitrogen to form magnesium nitride; a stubborn residue, roughly one part in a hundred, refused to react no matter how long the process ran. Rayleigh, working by electric sparking in the presence of alkali — the same method Henry Cavendish had used in 1785, when he too had been left with an unidentified residue of about a hundredth of his original air and had gone no further with it — got the same intractable remainder. The two men announced their new gas, which they named argon after the Greek for idle, at the British Association meeting in August 1894, and set out the full case in a paper read to the Royal Society the following January, giving it a density close to one and a half times that of nitrogen and an atomic weight near forty.

What they had found was not merely a new substance but one that violated the organising principle of the table that was supposed to classify it. Mendeleev’s periodic law sorted elements by valence — their combining power with hydrogen, oxygen and chlorine — arranged in ascending order of atomic weight within columns built from that chemistry. Argon combined with nothing. Its valence was zero, a value the table’s column structure had no provision for, because every column before 1894 was defined by some positive combining behaviour. Worse, its atomic weight did not even fall into an empty slot: potassium sat at thirty-nine and calcium at forty, with no gap between them for a third element to occupy. The successes that had made the periodic law famous were gaps filled correctly in advance — gallium, germanium and scandium had each been specified, down to the approximate atomic weight and physical properties, years before anyone isolated them. Argon offered no gap and no valence group. It was, as the chemist Carmen Giunta later put it, the piece that would not fit.

Mendeleev’s first response, published in Nature in 1895, was not to revise the table but to deny that the piece existed. He argued that argon was most plausibly a triatomic form of nitrogen, N3, on the model of ozone’s relationship to ordinary oxygen, O3 — a molecule rather than an element, with a molecular weight near forty-two that would leave the periodic system wholly untouched. It was not a frivolous suggestion; allotropy was an established phenomenon, and treating a stubborn gas as a disguised form of something already classified was exactly the kind of move the system was built to reward. It was also testable, and it failed the test. Rayleigh and Ramsay measured the ratio of argon’s specific heats at constant pressure and constant volume and got 1.67, the value kinetic theory predicts for a gas whose molecules are single atoms; no triatomic molecule, nitrogen’s or anything else’s, produces that number. Argon was not disguised nitrogen. It was something the table had not made room for.

The room was not made until the objection became harder to sustain by force of numbers rather than argument. Ramsay, searching a uranium mineral called cleveite for more argon, instead isolated helium, previously known only from the sun’s spectrum; by 1898 he had added neon, krypton and xenon, a family of chemically inert gases spanning a wide range of atomic weights, all behaving exactly as argon did. One anomalous gas might be dismissed as an experimental artefact or a disguised allotrope. Five, in good periodic order, could not be. By 1902 Mendeleev accepted them, and the table gained a new column — labelled group zero for the valence it recorded — inserted between the halogens and the alkali metals, a slot the original logic of the table had never specified and could not have specified, since nothing in a system built entirely from combining power predicts the existence of elements defined by the absence of it.

The obvious objection is that this still counts as a vindication of sorts: once the column existed, it behaved with the same periodicity as every other column, ordering helium, neon, argon, krypton and xenon correctly by atomic weight, and later supplying the right slot, below xenon, for radon before its chemistry was fully characterised. A system flexible enough to absorb an entirely unanticipated class of matter without breaking is not a trivial achievement, and it is fair to credit the table with that absorption. But absorption after the fact is not the same accomplishment as the predictions that built the table’s reputation. Gallium, germanium and scandium were each named, by weight and by expected property, from gaps the table’s existing structure already implied before any sample of the element existed. No reading of the periodic law before 1894 implied that elements of zero valence ought to exist at all, let alone at which weights. The table did not foresee group zero. It was amended to survive it, and the amendment held only because a further puzzle in argon’s own atomic weight — higher than potassium’s, even though argon’s properties placed it a column earlier — was itself only reconciled a generation on, once Moseley’s atomic numbers gave chemists a way to order elements that did not depend on weight at all.

That distinction matters for more than this one episode, because the periodic law is routinely cited as the paradigm case of a scientific theory earning trust through successful prediction, and the noble gases are routinely folded into that same story as one more confirmation. They are not. They are the case in which the theory’s organising assumption — that chemical identity is combining behaviour — met a class of matter for which combining behaviour was precisely the property missing, and the fix was structural surgery, not foresight. A law that classifies everything it was built to classify, and then has to be rebuilt once something arrives that it was not built to classify, has shown something narrower than the triumphalist version of its own history admits.
