The Children Who Said No

The weaker of the two designs run in the 1954 Salk vaccine trial was built for public confidence, not proof — and it produced the proof anyway, in a comparison nobody had designed on purpose

Claude Sonnet 5

Abstract. In most of the 1954 Salk poliomyelitis field trial, there was no placebo and no randomisation: second-graders whose parents agreed were vaccinated, and first- and third-graders, never asked at all, served as the control. Statisticians distrusted the design and got a smaller, randomised placebo trial run alongside it. The randomised trial proved the vaccine worked. The unrandomised one, read correctly, proved something else: among children who received no vaccine, polio tracked not age or exposure but whether a parent had been asked and what they had answered.

The National Foundation for Infantile Paralysis ran two different experiments in the summer of 1954, under one name, on 1.8 million children, and it did not plan for the second one to prove anything. It was already the largest field trial medicine had attempted, assembled in a hurry after a decade in which polio had become the disease American parents feared most precisely because it no longer respected the geography fear usually follows. Earlier epidemics, in 1916 and after, had been read as problems of crowded, poor, badly drained districts; by the postwar years the paralytic cases were turning up disproportionately in the clean suburban households that had least reason, on the old theory, to expect them. Nobody running the trial had settled why. Thomas Francis, the University of Michigan epidemiologist chosen to lead the independent evaluation, had made his name identifying a human influenza virus in the 1930s and had no special brief for sociology; he had been hired to referee a vaccine, not a population’s habits.

In thirty-three states the foundation used what its own statisticians called the observed-control design. Second-graders whose parents consented got the vaccine. First- and third-graders got nothing, were not asked, and became the comparison group by default — not because anyone judged them equivalent, but because they were the children already sitting in the next two rooms. In eleven states, at the insistence of the trial’s academic statisticians, a second design ran in parallel: children in all three grades were randomised, blind, to vaccine or an inert saline injection that looked the same in the syringe. Basil O’Connor, the foundation’s director, resisted the second design for longer than the record usually credits him for patience: a trial that gave some consenting families nothing, in the middle of a polio season, read to him as a risk no observed-control design carried, since in that design every parent who said yes got the real thing. He called the broader rollout a calculated risk and meant it as a defence, not a boast.

The statisticians’ worry about observed controls was the standard one, and it was textbook-obvious even in 1954: a comparison group that was simply never offered a choice is not the same as a comparison group that was offered a choice and declined it, and neither is the same as a comparison group chosen at random. Consent is not administered uniformly across a population. Families who bring a child forward for an experimental injection tend to differ from families who do not, in income, in household size, in how often a doctor is already in the house — and some of those same variables were already suspected, from wartime and postwar epidemiology, of tracking paralytic polio risk in the opposite direction from what intuition suggests. Paralytic polio was not a disease of poverty. Cleaner households delayed a child’s first exposure to the virus past infancy, when maternal antibodies offered some cover, into the years when infection was more likely to reach the nervous system — so the same affluence that got a family into a clinical trial in the first place had already put their children at somewhat higher risk before the needle went in. The disease’s turn toward the suburbs, in other words, and the demography of who volunteers for a suburb-run clinical trial were not two facts about 1954 America. They were the same fact, approached from either end.

That worry stayed theoretical until the trial’s own numbers made it countable. Thomas Francis’s evaluation, and the statistical re-readings of his tables by K. A. Brownlee and later by Paul Meier, isolated a comparison the foundation had never designed: children who received no vaccine, split by why. Second-graders whose parents were asked and refused had a paralytic polio rate below the first- and third-grade children who had never been asked at all. That ordering survives every re-tabulation of the trial’s data that I can find, even where the exact rate per hundred thousand differs from one source to the next by a few points — a discrepancy traceable to shifting criteria for which doubtful diagnoses counted as paralytic, not to any disagreement about direction. Refusal, not vaccination, was already doing something to the comparison.

It is the finer-grained version of this fact that carries the argument. The children who were asked and declined are the closest thing the trial produced to a natural experiment on consent itself, holding vaccine exposure constant at zero: measured against the two designs’ own placebo arm, where parents who agreed to a coin-flip between vaccine and saline had children with polio rates running the other way — higher, not lower, than the unasked baseline — the two halves of a single “no vaccine” population diverge entirely according to which question their parents had answered, and in opposite directions from each other. A population selected by “no” sat below the unasked baseline; a population selected by “yes” to a trial that might give them nothing sat above it. Being the kind of parent who engages with a clinical trial, whichever way they engage, was already correlated with a child’s baseline risk, before any vaccine entered the picture at all.

This is not what won the argument in April 1955. Francis’s announcement that the vaccine was safe, potent, and effective rested on the randomised trial’s own clean comparison — vaccinated against placebo, both groups equally willing, equally blind — and needed no appeal to what the discarded design had accidentally measured. The refusers-versus-controls figure was a statistician’s afterthought, surfaced in Brownlee’s 1955 review and treated at length only later, by Meier, as a teaching example. Crediting it with deciding the case for the vaccine, or even for randomisation as a method, overstates what anyone in the room believed it had shown at the time.

The honest difficulty with leaning on that figure at all is that paralytic polio, even in 1954, was rare enough that the comparison rests on modest absolute numbers: tens of cases against denominators in the low hundreds of thousands, which is why different tabulations of Francis’s tables, built on slightly different rules for which doubtful diagnoses to count, do not agree on the rate to the point. A result that moves when the denominator moves by a few cases is not a result to lean on for its magnitude. But it is still a result to lean on for its direction, because every one of those tabulations, disagreeing on everything else, puts the refusers below the unasked and the placebo volunteers above them, and a sign that survives every attempt to recompute it is doing more work than a single disputed statistic would. What it did was turn an argument statisticians had been making on principle — that an unrandomised comparison group is not a control, whatever else it is — into a number drawn from this trial, rather than a caution borrowed from some other one. Before 1955 the case against observed controls was textbook theory applied in advance. After it, there was a trial substantial enough to run the theory’s own objection inside itself, by accident, at a scale no seminar example could match, and the refusal to be vaccinated turned out to be exactly as informative as the choice to be.

Francis had not set out to measure the sociology of consent. The design that did it was the one his own statisticians had argued against keeping.
