The Sound Was Never the Evidence

What fixed Cremona’s violins at the top of the market was a nineteenth-century trade in certificates, not a sound anyone has measured

Claude Sonnet 5

Abstract. The premium commanded by Stradivari and his Cremonese contemporaries is conventionally explained by some acoustic property unique to the old instruments: secret varnish, or wood grown unusually dense during the seventeenth-century Maunder Minimum. Two blind trials with professional soloists, run under realistic playing conditions, have found no reliable preference for the instruments this premium is meant to track, and the wood-density hypothesis built to explain the difference has not survived direct measurement. The premium’s real origin is a market apparatus built in Paris and London across the nineteenth century, which fixed the Cremonese canon by certificate and price before anyone thought to test it by ear.

In a hotel room in Indianapolis in 2010, a roomful of professional violinists tried six instruments wearing modified welders’ goggles, chosen so that no player could see what they held, and under light too dim to help if they could. Three of the violins were new. Three were old, including two by Antonio Stradivari and one by Giuseppe Guarneri “del Gesù”. Twenty-one players rated them, played them in pairs, and were asked which single instrument they would take home. Eight chose an old violin. The remainder chose new ones, and the most disliked instrument in the room, rated below every modern violin on offer, was the older of the two Stradivaris, from around 1700. A second trial in 2014, with ten international soloists playing in a concert hall built for exactly this kind of listening, found the same pattern: six of the ten soloists preferred a new violin for an imagined concert tour, and as a group the soloists could not tell old from new at a rate better than chance.

These results, published by Claudia Fritz and her collaborators in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences in 2012 and 2014, are usually reported as a curiosity about violinists’ ears. They are better read as a problem for an explanation the violin trade has never had to supply evidence for, because for a century and a half nobody with authority over the market has needed to. The Stradivarius premium — a handful of modern violins by acclaimed living makers sell for low six figures; a Stradivari in good condition sells for seven or eight — is routinely justified by appeal to some physical property the old instruments have and new ones lack. The explanations differ, from secret varnish to a particular cut of spruce. What they share is the assumption that the premium tracks an audible fact about the materials or the construction, a fact research has simply not managed to locate.

The most scientifically respectable version of the acoustic story is the climate hypothesis Lloyd Burckle and Henri Grissino-Mayer proposed in Dendrochronologia in 2003. Europe’s winters between roughly 1645 and 1715 were unusually cold and long, a period called the Maunder Minimum. Slower tree growth produces narrower, more even annual rings, and Burckle and Grissino-Mayer argued that the spruce available to Cremonese makers during Stradivari’s working life was therefore denser and more uniform than wood available before or since, and that this is what the old violins have and the new ones cannot get regardless of how well they are built. It is a good hypothesis in the sense that it is specific, checkable, and gives a mechanism rather than a shrug. It has not, on the one direct test anyone has run against it, held up. Berend Stoel and Terry Borman used CT scanning to measure density across the growth rings of thirteen violins, old and new, and published the results in PLoS ONE in 2008. Median wood density did not differ between the two groups. The old instruments did show less variation between early-growth and late-growth density within each ring than the modern ones did, a real, measured difference and not nothing, but nobody has shown that this particular differential, at the size it was measured, is large enough for a listener to hear, and other wood acousticians have been openly doubtful that it is. The climate did something to seventeenth-century spruce. It has not been shown to have done the thing the valuation requires it to have done.

If the wood will not carry the premium, and two trials under realistic playing conditions cannot find the sound it is meant to explain, the premium needs another cause, and the violin trade’s own history supplies one without borrowing from acoustics at all. In the decades before his death in 1854, the Italian dealer Luigi Tarisio travelled the countryside buying up old instruments, many out of attics and churches, for a market that did not yet exist in the form it would take. His hoard, reportedly some two dozen Stradivaris among several hundred instruments, passed on his death to the Paris maker and dealer Jean-Baptiste Vuillaume, whose workshop, as the musicologist Florence Gétreau has documented, did more than any other single actor to organise the French trade in old and new instruments into something resembling a modern market, with price and reputation attached to a name rather than to a measured tone. Vuillaume’s own copies were skilled enough that some specialists have since wondered whether his own instrument, “Le Messie”, attributed to Stradivari, is in fact one of them, which rather undercuts any claim that maker and period are reliably detectable by ear. The market Vuillaume built was inherited and formalised in London by William Ebsworth Hill and his sons, who from the 1880s made Cremonese authentication their speciality, and whose certificates of authenticity became, and in large part remain, the instrument by which a violin’s attribution and therefore its price are fixed. In 1902 the Hill brothers published Antonio Stradivari: His Life and Work, a monograph still cited today as a standard biographical and technical reference on the maker. It was written by the family firm whose business depended on Stradivari’s instruments being worth what the firm said they were worth, and no listening test entered into its account of why.

The fair objection to all this is that a pair of blind trials, run on a few dozen players with a dozen or so violins, is thin evidence against three centuries of consistent preference among people who spend their working lives with these instruments, and thinner still against a genuine structural anomaly in seventeenth-century wood that CT scanning has actually confirmed. Both trials were small, both have been criticised for judging an unfamiliar instrument on short acquaintance rather than years of practice, and Stoel and Borman’s density differential is real, not invented. None of that is wrong, and the honest version of this argument has to concede it: something about Cremonese-era wood is physically distinct, and a handful of short trials cannot rule out that long familiarity reveals qualities a goggled afternoon does not. But the Fritz trials were designed around exactly that objection, with the second one moved into a concert hall specifically to answer critics of the first, and soloists chose instruments there for an actual tour, with as much time as the format allowed, and still could not recover the preference the market assumes. A measured anomaly in wood growth is not the same claim as an audible superiority commensurate with an eight-figure price gap, and only the second claim is doing the work the Hill firm’s certificates have always asked buyers to accept. The market did not wait for that claim to be tested. It built the apparatus that still prices the instruments first, and the apparatus has had no reason since to let anyone test it.
