The Novel Weapon That Survived Its Own Autopsy

Spotted knapweed’s chemical identity collapsed under the critics it attracted; the field test built to settle the question did not

Claude Sonnet 5

Abstract. In 2003 ecologists proposed that spotted knapweed invades Montana rangeland by exuding a root chemical, (−)-catechin, against which resident plants have no evolved defence. A sequence of papers from 2005 to 2009, several from the same laboratory, found the chemical largely absent from field soil and too unstable to explain the damage first reported, and the novel-weapons hypothesis is now treated as a cautionary tale about mechanism announced before it was checked. The one experiment built to test the hypothesis itself, rather than its chemistry, still found the asymmetry it predicted. The molecule failed. The pattern did not.

In 2003 a team led by the ecologist Ragan Callaway reported that spotted knapweed clears native bunchgrass from Montana rangeland by poisoning the ground beneath it. The agent they named was (−)-catechin, a compound exuded from the weed’s roots in concentrations, by their measurement, high enough to damage the roots of resident grasses that had no evolutionary history of encountering it (Bais et al. 2003). A year later, Callaway and Wendy Ridenour generalised the finding into the novel-weapons hypothesis: an invader succeeds, on this account, not by being a better competitor in the abstract but by carrying a biochemical weapon that the recipient community has never evolved a defence against, because it never needed one (Callaway & Ridenour 2004).

The hypothesis entered a field that already had a leading rival. Bernd Blossey and Rolf Nötzold had argued a few years earlier that invaders succeed because release from the specialist herbivores of their home range lets them shift resources from chemical defence into growth, becoming better competitors through evolution rather than through armament (Blossey & Nötzold 1995). Where that account locates the advantage in what the invader stopped spending, novel weapons located it in what the invader brought with it: a chemical its new neighbours had no coevolutionary reason to resist. The two were not mutually exclusive, but they pointed experiments in different directions, and catechin was the cleanest test case novel weapons had, because it was falsifiable in a way few ecological mechanisms are: a named compound, in a measurable tissue, doing a specific and disprovable thing, rather than a redistribution of resources that shows up only in aggregate growth curves years after the fact.

Each of those tests was run, mostly by researchers sympathetic to the hypothesis, and each went worse for catechin than the 2003 paper had suggested it would. Blair and colleagues, using chromatography more sensitive than the earlier work, assayed individual plants and found production two orders of magnitude below what had been reported, and surveyed soil at two Montana sites without detecting any (Blair et al. 2005). Perry and colleagues then ran the largest field survey the question had received: four hundred and two soil samples from eleven spotted-knapweed sites across a full year, finding pure catechin only rarely present overall, though the sites where it did turn up held concentrations comparable to the earlier high reports, which kept the compound from being dismissed outright even as its reliability collapsed (Perry et al. 2007). Stephen Duke’s group went further, arguing from the compound’s instability in soil and its inconsistent appearance across sites that catechin is probably not a weapon of any kind in this system, novel or otherwise, and that whatever mechanism is doing the work has not yet been found (Duke et al. 2009).

By 2009 the chemical story that had made the 2003 paper famous had been dismantled largely by people who had helped build it, which is a more severe test than any outside critic could have supplied. The textbook treatment of the episode since has tended to flatten this into a single lesson: a striking molecular mechanism, announced in a major journal, collapsed on replication, and invasion biology should be more careful about naming its weapons before it has found them. That lesson is fair as far as it goes, and it understates nothing about the chemistry.

It does, however, run past a result that does not fit it. In the same window as the critiques, Lauren Thorpe and colleagues ran the experiment the novel-weapons hypothesis actually needed and had not yet received: not a soil assay but a field test of the biological claim itself, applying the same root exudate to plant communities in both the weed’s native range in Romania and its invaded range in Montana, in the same two years, using the same protocol (Thorpe et al. 2009). If the hypothesis is right for the reason it gives, the exudate should suppress growth where the community has no shared history with it and do little where resistance has had time to evolve. That is what they found. The exudate reduced growth among Montana natives in both years, with the size of the effect varying by species; among Romanian natives, sharing evolutionary history with the knapweed, it did close to nothing. This is the asymmetry the hypothesis was built to predict, observed where prediction is hardest to fake: in the ground, across two growing seasons, on two continents, not in a dish.

The honest difficulty, and it deserves stating as starkly as the critics stated it, is that the Thorpe result and the chemistry result cannot both be given full weight as stories about Montana today. If catechin is rarely present in the field and unstable where it is, as Blair’s, Perry’s and Duke’s work indicates, then an experiment that applies catechin directly to field plots is not quite testing what spotted knapweed is doing to its neighbours right now; it is testing what would happen if the compound were present at the concentration applied, which the preceding decade of soil chemistry says it usually is not. Duke’s group is explicit that this gap is unresolved, and no paper in the sequence closes it. A sceptic is entitled to say that Thorpe’s result confirms only that catechin is toxic to naive plants under laboratory-grade dosing, a fact nobody disputed, and that it says nothing about the actual mechanism operating in the field.

That objection is right about the gap and wrong about what follows from it. What collapsed between 2005 and 2009 was a specific empirical claim: that catechin reaches phytotoxic concentrations in ordinary field soil, reliably enough to be the mechanism. What did not collapse, because no paper was built to touch it until Thorpe’s group ran theirs, is the structural claim beneath it: that a resident community can be asymmetrically vulnerable to a biochemical novelty it has not coevolved with, in a way a community with shared history is not. Those are two different claims, the second resting on the first only for its proposed chemical identity, not for its logical content, and the evidence that falsified the lower one says nothing against the upper one. The field’s habit of citing the whole sequence as a single cautionary tale, chemical and hypothesis together, treats a structural prediction as refuted by a measurement problem in the one compound chosen to carry it. The search for whatever does reach phytotoxic concentrations in Montana’s soil is still open. The pattern it would need to produce has already been found.
