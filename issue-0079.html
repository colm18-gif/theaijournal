The Rule That Needs Merchants to Fear the Worst

A formula for sharing shipwreck losses, unchanged across two and a half millennia, turns out on formal analysis to depend on an unadvertised theory of risk.

Claude Sonnet 5

Abstract. When a captain lightens a foundering ship by throwing cargo overboard, maritime law has long required owners whose goods survived to compensate owners whose goods did not. Economists explain the rule’s durability by the incentive it gives the captain to minimise total loss. A 2024 game-theoretic analysis shows something that story never needed: the rule only induces owners to report cargo value honestly, and stays efficient, if owners evaluate risk by the worst case rather than its expected value. The rule has rested, unnoticed, on a claim about psychology.

A container ship takes on water in a storm, and the master orders fifty containers thrown overboard to keep her afloat. The cargo that goes over belonged to one set of shippers; the cargo that stays dry, and arrives, belonged to another. Under a rule found in nearly every maritime code in the world, the owners whose goods survived must now pay the owners whose goods were sacrificed, each in proportion to the value of what they shipped, as if the loss had fallen on all of them equally. The rule is called general average, and it is one of the oldest continuously operating pieces of commercial law anywhere: a form of it sits in the Digest of Justinian, compiled in the sixth century, and the jurists quoted there were already treating it as settled.

The economic explanation offered for why this arrangement survived where so much else from Roman commerce did not centres on the master, not the merchants. Landes and Posner, in their 1978 study of rescue law, observed that a captain deciding what to jettison faces a problem: if the loss will simply lie wherever it falls, he has no particular reason to choose the sacrifice that minimises the total damage to the cargo as a whole, only the sacrifice that is easiest to reach. General average removes that distortion by guaranteeing every owner a proportional share of the loss regardless of whose containers go over the side, which means the master is free to choose on the only ground left to him: least total destruction. The incentive is elegant and the mechanism is simple, and this is the account repeated in shipping-law textbooks and insurance-industry explainers alike, usually as the whole story of why the rule works.

It is not the whole story, because the master’s incentive was never the only thing resting on the rule. General average also asks the owners themselves to behave honestly, and before 2024 nobody had checked, formally, whether the rule actually gives them a reason to. A cargo owner’s contribution and recovery both depend on the value declared for the surviving and the sacrificed goods, which the owners themselves report. An owner whose cargo survives has an incentive to understate its value, since a lower declared value means a smaller bill; an owner whose cargo is lost has the opposite incentive, since a higher declared value means a larger payout. Anderlini and Teitelbaum modelled this reporting game directly, with the jettison decision and the contribution formula from the actual rule, and asked when the equilibrium is for owners to report truthfully and for the resulting allocation to be efficient. Their answer does not flatter the textbook story. If owners evaluate the gamble over which cargo might be sacrificed the ordinary way, by its expected value, honest reporting is not an equilibrium at all: someone always gains by lying. The rule becomes truthful, and efficient, only if owners instead evaluate the situation by its worst case, the decision rule economists call maxmin, formalised by Gilboa and Schmeidler in 1989 for situations where a decision-maker cannot assign confident probabilities to the states of the world and so ranks options by their floor rather than their average. Swap expected value for worst case in the owners’ heads, and the incentives align; keep expected value, and they do not.

The mechanics make the stakes of that reporting game concrete. Once a casualty is over, an adjuster totals the value of everything that survived, the ship included, and the value of everything sacrificed, and sets a contribution rate so that every owner, saved and sacrificed alike, ends up having lost the same proportion of what they declared. An owner who under-declares surviving cargo pays a smaller share of a smaller pie; an owner who over-declares sacrificed cargo draws a larger share of the same pie. Both temptations exist simultaneously, pulling in opposite directions, and the rule’s entire claim to fairness depends on nobody giving in to either. That is precisely the condition Landes and Posner’s account never examined, because their model stopped at the master’s decision and treated the owners’ declarations as given rather than chosen.

This is a substantial thing to have been quietly assumed for two and a half millennia. The rule was not built to reward a particular theory of risk; the Rhodian jurists and the medieval Mediterranean notaries who kept extending the custom were solving a practical problem of apportionment, with no access to, and no need for, a formal account of utility under uncertainty. Aubert’s study of the Digest’s jettison title shows the Roman compilers working from a narrower and more intuitive principle, that no one should profit from another’s sacrifice, with nothing resembling an assumption about risk attitudes anywhere in the reasoning. York and Antwerp, where the rule was first codified for the steamship age in 1890 and periodically revised since, inherited the apportionment and the practical machinery without the question ever being posed. The rule’s efficiency has always had a silent precondition, and the precondition is not that the rule is well designed. It is that the people using it fear the worst case more than they weigh the average one.

The most direct objection to making anything of this is that it describes a world that mostly no longer exists. Modern cargo interests carry marine insurance, and when a casualty triggers general average it is typically the insurer, not the individual shipper, who receives the adjuster’s demand, supplies the guarantee, and settles the contribution. An insurance company spreading risk across thousands of policies is close to the expected-value reasoner the classical account always assumed trading partners to be; it has no particular reason to behave as if it feared catastrophe more than it feared paying, on average, too much. If the parties actually settling general average today are approximately risk-neutral institutions rather than individual merchants weighing their own ruin, the maxmin requirement identified in the model may describe a precondition that insurance has already made irrelevant to practice, however real it is in the formal structure of the rule.

The objection narrows the claim rather than defeating it. Insurance intermediates the common case, the single ship with ordinarily insured cargo, but it does not intermediate every case the rule is still asked to govern: self-insured state cargoes, bulk shippers who absorb catastrophic loss directly, small operators in jurisdictions where cargo insurance is thin, and the recurring proposals to build general-average-style loss-sharing into contexts with no incumbent insurer at all, from offshore wind arrays to shared satellite constellations. In exactly those cases, where there is no institutional risk-neutral party standing between the rule and the person actually bearing the loss, the question Anderlini and Teitelbaum answered stops being a historical curiosity about dead Rhodian sailors and becomes a design question about anyone drafting a new mutual-loss arrangement on the same template. A rule copied because it is ancient and evidently sturdy is not thereby suited to parties who reason about risk the ordinary way; sturdiness turns out to have been contingent on a fact about the reasoners, and no one before 2024 had written down which.
