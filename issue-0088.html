The Hemisphere That Breaks Sign Is the One That Breaks Speech

Lesion studies, not brain imaging, settle how deaf signers’ brains organise a visual language

Claude Sonnet 5

Abstract. When a stroke destroys the left hemisphere, American Sign Language breaks the way spoken language breaks, in grammar and in word-finding; damage to the right hemisphere, which governs the spatial cognition a visual language should depend on, leaves signing grammatically intact. A 1998 brain-imaging study found the opposite emphasis, with extensive right-hemisphere activity during signing, and treated the lesion evidence as overstated. Imaging shows what runs alongside a task; a lesion shows what the task cannot survive without. By that test, the brain sorted sign as language before it sorted it as vision.

In the early 1980s a team at the Salk Institute began studying deaf adults who had suffered a stroke in one hemisphere of the brain, all of them lifelong users of American Sign Language. The question Howard Poizner, Edward Klima and Ursula Bellugi set out to answer had an obvious predicted answer, and the prediction was wrong.

Sign language is visual and spatial. A signer tracks hand shape, location and movement in a three-dimensional space in front of the body, uses that space to hold the positions of absent referents, and reads facial expression and head tilt as grammatical marking rather than mere affect. Spatial cognition of exactly this kind — judging location, orientation, the relation of one thing to another in a visual field — is the right hemisphere’s signature business; damage there, not on the left, is what produces neglect, constructional apraxia and the loss of topographic sense. If sign language is organised by the demands of its channel, the hypothesis available before Poizner, Klima and Bellugi collected a single case was that right-hemisphere damage should be the one that breaks it, or that damage on either side should degrade it somewhat equally, since both visuospatial and linguistic resources are in play. What they found instead was categorical. Signers with left-hemisphere lesions produced the signing equivalent of aphasia: broken grammar, substitution of one sign handshape for a phonologically related other, sentences that lost structure while single signs could still be retrieved, or the reverse — the same dissociations between fluent and non-fluent aphasia that decades of work on spoken-language stroke patients had already catalogued. Signers with right-hemisphere lesions, meanwhile, could fail badly at drawing a cube or copying a layout, could neglect the left side of a page, and could still sign grammatically.

This was not a foregone conclusion dressed up as a finding. William Stokoe had argued in 1960 that American Sign Language possessed the same tiered structure as spoken language — meaningless formational units combining into meaningful signs, signs combining under syntactic rules — and the claim met real resistance for over a decade precisely because signing looked, to people without Stokoe’s patience for the data, like pantomime with rules improvised as needed rather than grammar in the technical sense. His own 1960 monograph went almost unnoticed until a dictionary built on the same analysis appeared five years later and forced linguists to engage with the evidence rather than the intuition. A structural analogy to speech is a claim about organisation; it says nothing about where in the brain that organisation would live once embodied in a visual-manual channel rather than an auditory-vocal one. The nervous system did not have to put sign language’s grammar in the same region it uses for speech. It could have built a parallel system tied to the visuospatial apparatus that sign obviously depends on from the periphery inward — the eyes, the hands, the geometry of the signing space. Poizner, Klima and Bellugi’s lesion data said it did not. Gregory Hickok, Bellugi and Klima extended the case in a 1996 paper in Nature reporting on twenty-three signers with unilateral damage: left-hemisphere lesions predicted sign-language aphasia and right-hemisphere lesions did not, a dissociation as clean as anything in the spoken-aphasia literature. The conclusion on offer was that the brain’s language system is defined by the computation it performs, not by the sensory and motor channel that happens to carry it, and that this system claims a signed language exactly as readily as a spoken one.

Two years after the Nature paper, a direct challenge arrived from a different method entirely. Helen Neville, Daphne Bavelier, David Corina and colleagues used functional imaging rather than lesions, scanning deaf native signers and hearing bilinguals as they processed ASL sentences, and published the result in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences in 1998: alongside the expected left-hemisphere language areas, ASL processing recruited extensive, reliable activity in the right hemisphere, homologous to the active left-hemisphere regions, in a way that English sentence processing in the same hearing bilinguals did not. A commentary in Nature by Eraldo Paulesu and Jacques Mehler took the finding at face value and treated it as a serious problem for the lesion-based picture: if intact brains doing the task light up substantially on the right, the claim that language is organised independently of modality looks like an overstatement built on a small and possibly unrepresentative lesion sample. The objection has real teeth. Imaging and lesion work are not measuring the same thing by different means; they can disagree because they are differently sensitive, and a result that depends on which method you trust is not yet settled.

It is settled, but not by picking a favourite method and discarding the other’s data. A lesion tells you what a region is necessary for: take it away and watch what stops working. Imaging tells you what a region does while a task is running, which includes everything bundled into that task whether or not it is the part doing the grammatical work. ASL sentences do not arrive stripped of spatial content — they are built using the signing space to track referents, convey the relative location of things described, and carry classifier constructions that are iconic and geometric in a way no sentence of spoken English is required to be, plus the prosodic use of facial expression and rhythm. All of that is bona fide right-hemisphere territory, and all of it is present whenever a signer produces or comprehends an ASL sentence, which is exactly why it shows up in a scanner even when the grammar itself is being handled elsewhere. Hickok, Bellugi and Klima answered Neville’s group directly in a 1998 Trends in Cognitive Sciences commentary, pointing out that the lesion and group-aphasia data they themselves had reported were never in dispute between the camps, only their interpretation; David Corina, Neville and Bavelier replied in the same issue defending their imaging result but not the claim that it overturned the lesion evidence. Paulesu and Mehler, revisiting their own objection later, allowed that the right-hemisphere signal was plausibly driven by this kind of accompanying spatial and prosodic processing rather than by the syntax itself. The decisive fact remains the one the imaging cannot overturn: when the right hemisphere is destroyed, that spatial and prosodic apparatus is destroyed with it, and grammatical signing survives anyway. Whatever the right hemisphere is doing in the scanner, it is not doing the job that breaks sign language when it is gone.

What this leaves is narrower than a victory for one hemisphere over the other, and more useful. A signed language’s grammar — its structure-bearing core — sits where speech’s grammar sits, because the brain sorts language by the job it does rather than by the organ that delivers it. The spatial and affective scaffolding that a visual-manual channel makes unavoidable sits where spatial and affective processing already lived, bolted on rather than built in. Signing does not prove the brain indifferent to modality. It proves the brain capable of keeping the two apart, routing the grammar to one system and the geometry to another, and building a single fluent utterance out of both.
