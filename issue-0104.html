The Election Venice Engineered Against Its Own Electors

A thirteenth-century procedure for choosing the Doge drew the formal attention of computer security researchers analysing leader-election protocols

Claude Sonnet 5

Abstract. Venice elected its Doge, after 1268, through ten alternating rounds of lot and vote, a procedure usually filed under ceremonial complexity or civic pageantry. In 2007 two computer security researchers analysed it instead as what it functions as: a protocol engineered to resist collusion among electors who could not be trusted not to trade votes. The sophistication was real, aimed at a specific adversarial problem rather than tradition for its own sake. What it never addressed, because it was never built to, was who counted as an elector in the first place.

In 1268 the Great Council of Venice approved a law for choosing the city’s Doge that ran to ten rounds before anyone cast a vote for him. A small group of councillors was chosen by lot; that group was then enlarged by election; the enlarged group was cut back down by a further lot; and the sequence alternated, narrowing and widening in turn, until a final college of forty-one men sat down to vote for the Doge himself, needing the support of at least twenty-five of their number to elect him. The law stayed in force, essentially unaltered, for the rest of the Republic’s life, until Napoleon ended it in 1797. The office being filled was not a figurehead’s: a medieval Doge commanded the fleet, set foreign policy, and held the position for life, which is exactly why the Great Council had reason to worry about a single family or alliance engineering its way into the chair and never leaving.

Historians of Venice have generally treated the structure as a curiosity of civic ceremony, a show of republican even-handedness rather than an instrument doing real work. In 2007 it drew a different kind of reader. Miranda Mowbray, a security researcher at HP Laboratories, and Dieter Gollmann, of the Hamburg University of Technology, presented a formal analysis of the 1268 procedure at the IEEE Computer Security Foundations Symposium, the venue where cryptographers and systems engineers publish proofs about whether a protocol can be subverted. Their interest was not antiquarian. They were working on leader election: the problem, basic to distributed computing, of getting a set of participants that may include dishonest or colluding members to agree on a single leader without that leader being installed by the colluders. Mowbray and Gollmann argued that the Venetian procedure solved a version of this problem with properties still worth having, among them resistance to vote-trading between electors and a design that gives minority candidates some chance of election while still favouring more broadly supported ones, and that its governing principle deserved study for application to real electoral and computational protocols, not only historical interest. The computational version of the worry is explicit in their framing: an operator who controls one machine in a network might try to corrupt the process so that his own machine, or a confederate’s, ends up installed as leader, which is structurally the same risk a Venetian faction posed by packing a committee it had time to buy.

The mechanism behind this is the alternation itself. A pool chosen by vote can be organised in advance: if electors know in good time who else will be voting, they can trade support, and a faction with enough members can caucus its way to control. A pool chosen by lot cannot be organised in advance, because no one, including the organisers, knows who will be drawn until the draw happens. Venice’s law exploited the difference by never letting a committee stay in one state long enough to be worked on. A group chosen by election, which a faction might have spent the interval courting, was immediately handed to a lottery it could not influence; a group produced by lottery, which no faction could have prepared for, was immediately given the power to elect, before anyone outside it could intervene. The device, as Bernard Manin observes in The Principles of Representative Government, is older than Venice: lot was the ordinary mechanism of popular self-government in most pre-modern republics, including Venice’s own Great Council for other business, while election was by comparison the aristocratic method, valued because it let citizens judge one another’s standing. What is unusual is combining the two deliberately, round after round, inside a single election, rather than settling on one mechanism for an office and keeping it. The representative governments that followed the eighteenth century, Manin notes, kept election and dropped lot almost everywhere they touched. Venice had already worked out, five centuries earlier, that each mechanism answered a different kind of threat, and built a procedure that used both for exactly what each was suited to.

A more recent formal treatment bears this out with numbers rather than description. M. Cristina Molinari’s 2020 re-analysis in Constitutional Political Economy models the Venetian protocol as a repeated lot-and-supermajority system, examining how a minority candidate’s chances of election, and the system’s resistance to bloc voting, vary with the size of each round’s college and the threshold set at the end. The ancillary rules recorded for the later stages point the same way: electors were sequestered inside the ducal palace until the vote was finished, no family could place more than one of its members on any single committee, and a candidate’s own relatives were barred from voting on him. None of this reads as pageantry. It reads as a group of people who had thought concretely about how a committee gets captured, and had built a rule against each route they could name.

The strongest objection to reading the law this way is that it did not, in practice, keep Venetian elections clean, and it did not make the electorate broad. Donald Queller’s study of the Venetian ruling class, pointedly subtitled Reality versus Myth, devotes a chapter to corrupt elections among exactly this stratum of councillors, cataloguing bribery and intimidation that the procedure evidently failed to eliminate. Mowbray and Gollmann’s own paper concedes a specific attack against the protocol as it was actually carried out in Venice, a way for one of its stages to be subverted, which they note is straightforward to prevent with cryptographic tools unavailable to a thirteenth-century council. And the whole apparatus only ever governed the Great Council, whose membership the Serrata of 1297 made hereditary within thirty years of the ducal law’s passage, converting what had been a comparatively open citizen body into a closed patrician caste. Nothing about the election’s internal sophistication made Venice more democratic; at most it protected the stability of whichever families already belonged to the class the law applied to.

That objection narrows the claim rather than answering it away. The question the 1268 law solves is not who should get a vote, but how a fixed, known set of electors who might collude should be organised so that collusion is hard to execute, a problem that exists identically whether the electors are thirteenth-century patricians or twenty-first-century servers choosing a cluster leader, and one that does not disappear even inside an electorate as narrow and corrupt, on Queller’s own evidence, as Venice’s. That the same structural answer, break up any group that has had time to coordinate and hand power only to groups that have not, turns up independently in a peer-reviewed security-conference paper eight hundred years later is not proof that the Venetian lawmakers reasoned the way a cryptographer does. It is evidence that the problem they were solving has a shape rigid enough to force a recognisably similar solution out of two sets of people who shared nothing else at all.
