Two uses of waste in the Yorkshire Domesday

Why a fiscal residual and a description of empty land were treated as interchangeable, and what the familiar map actually shows

Grok 4

Abstract. The Yorkshire folios of Great Domesday use wasta in two unmarked ways: as a description of unstocked land and as an accounting residual where the commissioners could not attach a holder, a geld figure or a value. The devastation map derived from those folios is an artefact of collapsing the two uses. The 1069–70 campaign is not in doubt; Circuit 6’s blanks are not a spatial record of it. Neighbouring circuits, which used the same Latin word more sparingly and more descriptively, make the incompatibility visible.

Circuit 6 of the Domesday inquest returned more land as wasta than any other circuit. In the Yorkshire folios the word sits over hundreds of vills, often with a blank where plough-teams, population and value TRE or TRW ought to stand, and the maps drawn from those blanks have been read as a geography of William I’s campaign in the winter of 1069–70. Darby and Maxwell plotted the word as if it marked a single condition of the soil, and the resulting blot across the North Riding and into the West remains the image most readers carry of the Harrying of the North. The reading works only if wasta is one thing. In these folios it is not.

The term is used, without any change of formula to warn the reader, in two ways that do not coincide. Sometimes it behaves like other manorial adjectives: it travels with remarks about restoration, with a partial recovery of value between 1066 and 1086, and with neighbouring vills that still have teams and sokemen. That is descriptive waste — land without stock, or land in trouble. Sometimes it does no such work. It heads long runs of nil returns, clustered by hundred and wapentake rather than by any reconstructed line of march, and it is thickest where Circuit 6’s entries are already most abbreviated. That is an accounting residual: a nil where the commissioners could not attach a holder, a geld assessment or a sworn value to the vill. The two jobs generate the same blank in the same column. A map of unmarked blanks is a map of neither job.

Wightman, who took the northern campaign more seriously than most later writers, had already been forced to split the Yorkshire entries. Some waste looked to him like empty land attributable to 1069–70; some looked like a formula covering tenurial reorganisation or a failure to complete the return. The concession matters, because once the word is allowed to do formulaic work the long sequences of nils stop being a delayed photograph of an army. They become a signature of how this circuit filled in a form. Roffe’s reconstruction of the inquest as a proceeding about geld and royal dues, rather than as a manorial inventory later copied into a book, supplies the mechanism. A vill returned as waste in that process need not have been empty. It may have been land for which no one answered, land taken out of assessment, land whose TRE holder was gone and whose TRW holder had not yet been named, or land whose value the hundred would not swear. Those are different facts. All of them leave the same cell blank. Darby’s cartography plots the cell.

The neighbouring circuits show the same Latin word doing less of the second job. In the Midlands waste is rarer, more often glossed, and more often paired with a reduced but non-zero value. That is overwhelmingly descriptive waste. Those circuits had geld lists in better order and a tenurial situation less chaotic sixteen years after the Conquest; they did not need wasta as a residual, and so they did not produce a blot. The contrast is circuit practice, not a measure of relative suffering. The North had been fought over — the fighting is not the issue — and Circuit 6 used one word to record both the fighting’s local effects and its own inability to complete a line. Those two causes are not marked in the text. They are therefore not separable on a map.

Palliser’s reading of the wider Yorkshire evidence is the right check on the cartography. Churches continue; place-names persist; later population figures do not describe a cleaned-out county. A desert in 1069 that was still a desert in 1086 is not what the non-Domesday record shows. Dalton, who does argue for prolonged and violent disruption in the making of the Yorkshire honours, still has to set waste beside value-change, tenure and the construction of lordship. He cannot let the word stand alone as a campaign plan. Once it cannot stand alone, the density of wasta in 1086 is no longer evidence of where the army went. It is evidence of where this circuit stopped being able to finish an entry.

What turns on the distinction is a habit of illustration, not a quarrel about whether William wasted the North. Descriptive waste can corroborate empty land; residual waste can corroborate only a gap in the geld. Treating the two as interchangeable borrows the chroniclers’ violence to interpret a spreadsheet they never saw, and then returns the spreadsheet as a picture of the violence. The picture is coherent only because the two uses have been collapsed.

The strongest objection comes at full strength. The independent narratives are not shy. The E-text of the Chronicle, Symeon of Durham and Orderic Vitalis describe a deliberate wasting of the North, with famine after; Orderic’s hundred thousand dead is incredible as a count but not as a claim of scale. If wasta in 1086 were merely a fiscal residual, why is it so much commoner in Yorkshire than in Kent, and why do the same vills so often lack teams, people and value together? A purely administrative account might explain the geography of the word at the scale of a circuit; it does not, on this view, explain it at the scale of the kingdom. A nil return that records that no one answered may itself be the footprint of killing and flight. To prise the two uses apart is then to impose a distinction the commissioners did not make and the land did not support — a diplomatist’s refinement that leaves the obvious reading standing.

The objection is right that the campaign happened, and right that some blanks are empty land. It is wrong that this saves the map. The claim is not that Yorkshire was unharmed, nor that wasta never means empty. It is that the word is doing two jobs in the same columns, that the jobs are unmarked, and that a map of unmarked blanks is not a map of either. Kent has little waste because it was not fought over in 1069 and because its circuit could complete the form. Yorkshire has much waste because both were true: it was fought over, and its circuit often could not complete the form. Where plough-teams and people are absent together with the word, empty land is a fair inference. Where the word sits over a vill that other evidence shows occupied, or over a run of entries whose abbreviation matches the circuit’s other economies, the blank is a residual. The narratives and the spreadsheet are different kinds of record. Agreeing with the narratives that a campaign of exceptional brutality took place does not licence reading Circuit 6’s residuals as its itinerary. The commissioners were not drawing a campaign map. They were trying, with uneven success, to make the North answer to the geld. Wasta is the record of that attempt, in two senses at once. The blot is a picture of the word.
