The Marriage the Comparison Was Never Able to Condemn

Four decades of studies pitting arranged marriages against love marriages have not produced the satisfaction gap either side expected

Claude Sonnet 5

Abstract. The assumption that choosing one’s own spouse produces a more satisfying marriage than accepting one’s family’s choice is treated, outside the sociology of South Asian households, as too obvious to need testing. The empirical literature that has actually tested it, from a widely cited 1982 crossover study to three-group comparisons of Indian and American couples, has never produced a reliable satisfaction gap in either direction. The assumption survives on its plausibility, not on evidence anyone has managed to produce.

Usha Gupta and Pushpa Singh gave husbands and wives in Rajasthan a scale built in the United States to measure romantic love, sorted the results by whether each marriage had been arranged by family or chosen by the couple, and published a result in 1982 that flattered neither side’s intuition. Couples married for love scored high in their first year together, in the region of seventy points out of a possible ninety-one on Zick Rubin’s Liking and Loving Scale, and by ten years had fallen to roughly forty. Couples whose marriages had been arranged started lower, in the high fifties, and climbed past sixty by the ten-year mark, ending close to where the love matches had begun. The two lines crossed. Secondary accounts of the study disagree even on how many couples produced them, putting the sample anywhere between twenty-five and a hundred, which should have been a warning sign about how much weight the result could bear. It became, regardless, the single most quoted finding in the entire comparative literature on arranged and love marriage, repeated in outlets from Scientific American to Psychology Today as proof that family-brokered matches catch up with and overtake the love matches the surrounding culture treats as the obvious choice.

The question has not stayed academic. Arranged marriage has not quietly given way to free choice even where economic development theory expected it to: Keera Allendorf and Roshan Pandian’s 2016 study of national Indian survey data found the practice declining only slowly between 1970 and 2012, with the fastest-growing category not autonomous love marriage but a hybrid in which parents and children choose jointly. Hundreds of millions of people are living out whichever answer this comparison gives, which is a reason to want the comparison to be right rather than merely memorable.

What the finding is rarely asked to do is survive contact with a second study. Myers, Madathil and Benshoff compared three groups in 2008: Asian Indians in arranged marriages living in India, Asian Indians in arranged marriages living in the United States, and Americans in marriages of choice. The groups differed sharply in what they considered important to a marriage, but not, by the more commonly cited reading of the result, in how satisfied they said they were with it. Pamela Regan and her students ran a smaller comparison in 2012, thirty Indian-Americans in love marriages against twenty-eight in arranged ones, and again found no measurable difference in love, satisfaction or commitment between the groups, though they were careful to note that fifty-eight people, all of them educated and thoroughly assimilated into American life, is not a population from which much can be generalised. Across every version of the comparison run since Gupta and Singh, the gap that the ambient Western assumption requires has not shown up again, not in favour of the love marriages and, with that one contested exception, not reliably in favour of the arranged ones either.

That absence is itself the interesting result, because it is not what either side’s theory of marriage predicts. The case for free choice assumes that two adults selecting each other on attraction and compatibility will do better than a match made by relatives who do not have to live inside it. The case for arranged marriage assumes the opposite: that infatuation is a poor predictor of a durable partnership, and that people who already know a candidate’s family, finances, health and reputation will choose better than two people besotted with each other after a few months. Both theories are coherent, and both predict a measurable gap in some direction, yet four comparisons across two countries and three decades have failed to find one reliably. What the data instead suggests is that courtship and arrangement are not producing good matches and bad matches at different rates. They are two different procedures for gathering the same information about a prospective spouse, front-loaded differently, arriving by different routes at outcomes that converge once the marriage has had time to settle. A family vetting a match collects information about reputation, finances, health and kin approval before the wedding and leaves romantic feeling to develop, or not, afterward, under conditions that make leaving costly. A couple who chooses for themselves collects the romantic feeling first and the rest of the information afterward, sometimes expensively, sometimes too late to prevent the disappointment that shows up in Gupta and Singh’s declining love-marriage curve, if that curve is real at all. Neither procedure obviously produces a worse marriage than the other; they simply spend the same fixed budget of information-gathering and emotional adjustment in a different order, and a satisfaction survey taken at a single point in a marriage’s life will catch each procedure at a different stage of paying that cost.

The comparison this literature relies on cannot bear nearly the weight either side wants to put on it, and that is the sharpest problem with everything above. These are not couples randomly assigned to a marriage type and then followed for ten years; they are self-selected groups who differed from each other before the wedding in ways that plausibly affect a satisfaction score regardless of how the spouse was found. A family that still arranges marriages, and adult children who still accept that arrangement, are more likely to come from a community with stronger norms against divorce, more enforceable kin approval of the match, and a greater practical cost to reporting dissatisfaction honestly, none of which is information about the marriage itself. Epstein, Pandit and Thakar re-examined the Gupta and Singh pattern in 2013 and found that in their own data the crossover correlation was driven in large part by three respondents, a reminder that a sample small enough to produce a striking headline is also small enough for a handful of unusual cases to manufacture the entire effect. None of the studies that followed solve the deeper problem: they compare existing arranged and love matches to each other, not a hypothetical in which the same couple had been assigned to the other procedure, and no ethics board will ever authorise that experiment. Nor does any of this speak to marriages contracted without the consent the word “arranged” implies in these studies; a family that vetoes its own veto and compels a match is doing something categorically different from the brokered introductions Gupta, Regan and the rest were measuring, and the comparative literature has nothing to say about it one way or the other.

What four decades of trying to find the gap actually licenses is a narrower claim than either side wants: that the work required to turn two strangers into a durable, tolerable marriage can be distributed differently between the couple and their families without the distribution by itself deciding whether the marriage succeeds. That is a smaller claim than “arranged marriages are happier” or “love marriages are happier.” It is also the only one the evidence, such as it is, has actually earned, and the one the assumption on either side has never had to argue for because nobody has made it answer to a study it might lose.
