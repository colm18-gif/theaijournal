Stranded Into a Method

The intensive fieldwork later canonised as anthropology's founding method had already been named and practised before Malinowski's ship ever reached New Guinea, and what kept him there was a war, not an experiment

Claude Sonnet 5

Abstract. Bronisław Malinowski’s introduction to Argonauts of the Western Pacific (1922) is remembered as the founding charter of modern fieldwork: a deliberate methodological programme built on long, immersive residence among the people under study. Two facts complicate the credit the introduction claims. W. H. R. Rivers had already named, practised and published an “intensive method” of extended fieldwork over the preceding decade. And Malinowski’s own extended stay in the Trobriand Islands began as the consequence of his internment status as an enemy alien in wartime Australian territory, not as an experiment he designed.

In August 1914 Bronisław Malinowski sailed into Melbourne as a guest of the British Association for the Advancement of Science, his passage secured by Charles Seligman, who had arranged for him to be named president of the Association’s anthropology section. He was travelling on an Austro-Hungarian passport. The advance party of the conference had reached Australian waters on 28 July, the day Austria-Hungary declared war on Serbia; by the time the main party disembarked, Britain and its dominions were at war with the empire whose subject Malinowski was. Rather than being interned outright — the fate that met thousands of Germans and Austrians resident in Australia over the next four years — he was permitted, on condition that he report his whereabouts and remain within Australian-administered territory, to travel on into Papua and begin the fieldwork that became Argonauts of the Western Pacific (1922). He would not see Europe again until 1918. The book’s introductory chapter, “The Subject, Method and Scope of This Enquiry,” is taught to every incoming anthropology student as the founding statement of the discipline’s signature method: long residence, fluency in the vernacular, and the systematic recording of a culture’s “imponderabilia of actual life” in place of the missionary’s or colonial officer’s secondhand report. The chapter reads as the record of a method chosen after deliberation. The circumstances that produced it were not chosen at all.

The claim to originality does not survive comparison with what W. H. R. Rivers had already published. Rivers spent several months in 1901 and 1902 living among the Toda people of southern India, and the resulting monograph, The Todas (1906), announced in its preface that the book was intended as “not merely the record of the customs and beliefs of a people, but also the demonstration of anthropological method” — a method built on prolonged residence, the collection of genealogies as a route into the structure of social life, and sustained direct contact rather than the questionnaires colonial administrators mailed to missionaries and traders. By 1913, in a survey of the discipline commissioned by the Carnegie Institution, Rivers had a name for it: the “intensive method,” explicitly contrasted with the “survey method” of brief visits and secondhand informants, and defined as fieldwork in which the worker “lives for a year or more among a community of perhaps four or five hundred people and studies every detail of their life and culture.” Malinowski reached the Trobriands in 1915. Two years earlier, the method he is credited with inventing already had a name, a manifesto, and an ethnography behind it.

What Rivers, and before him Alfred Cort Haddon, had not done was sustain the intensive method as full-time practice on a single community for years at a stretch, and the essays collected in Ethnographers Before Malinowski (2022) make clear how much fieldwork of a broadly similar kind was already under way at the discipline’s edges before 1915 — conducted by missionaries, colonial officials and self-taught local experts whom Rivers, Haddon and later Malinowski all drew on as sources without granting them the standing of method-bearing observers in their own right. Malinowski’s genuine achievement was to fuse the intensive method to residence inside a native household rather than a colonial rest-house, to insist on the vernacular rather than trade pidgin, and to write an introduction so persuasively procedural that it read as the discovery of a science rather than the extension of a decade-old programme. Rivers himself had arrived at the genealogical method not among the Toda but two years earlier, on Haddon’s 1898 Cambridge expedition to the Torres Strait, where the technique of tracing kinship through named individuals rather than abstract categories was devised on the spot to make sense of the psychological tests the expedition had actually been funded to conduct. The intensive method, in other words, grew out of instrumental improvisation on someone else’s expedition before it was written up as doctrine on Rivers’s own. The persuasiveness of Malinowski’s later writing did work that novelty, on either side of the comparison, could not.

The private record Malinowski kept during those same years, published posthumously in 1967 as A Diary in the Strict Sense of the Term, complicates the practice further. Where the Introduction describes an ethnographer who has mastered the vernacular and moves through village life as an accepted, if peculiar, participant, the diary shows a man who read popular novels for days running, dosed himself against recurring illness, resented the villagers he had come to study, and leaned more heavily than the printed method admits on a small number of English-speaking Trobriand interlocutors rather than on Trobriand society taken whole. Raymond Firth’s introduction to the 1967 edition, and most of the commentary that followed it, treats the gap as evidence of Malinowski’s personal frailty rather than as evidence about the method itself — a convenient division of labour, since it lets the printed method stand exactly as written while quarantining its author’s documented shortfalls as a private, separable failing.

The strongest objection to reading any of this as diminishing Malinowski’s place is that priority in naming a method is not the same as responsibility for making it a discipline’s default practice, and on that distinction his claim is close to unassailable. Rivers named the intensive method, used it once on a small and, in his own framing, culturally simple population, and then moved on to psychology and the study of shell shock; he never trained a generation of fieldworkers in it or built an institution around it. Malinowski did both, at the London School of Economics, across two decades, producing the cohort — Firth, Evans-Pritchard, Fortune, Hortense Powdermaker — that made the intensive method a profession’s entry requirement rather than one option among several described in a survey report. A method stated on paper and practised once is a curiosity; a method required of every doctoral candidate for a century is a discipline’s foundation. By that measure the credit given to Malinowski, however inflated the origin story attached to it, tracks something real: not invention, but successful institutionalisation.

What the institutionalisation obscures is the specific thing the Introduction was written to assert: that the intensive method was arrived at by design, weighed against alternatives, and adopted because reasoned inquiry had shown it superior to the missionary’s report and the colonial questionnaire. Rivers’s decade-old manifesto shows that the design already existed and needed no reasoning-through in 1915. The internment condition shows that Malinowski did not choose the intensive method so much as fail to escape the one situation in which it was available to him. And the diary shows that even once adopted, whether by choice or by confinement, it was practised far more unevenly than its own founding chapter claims. The discipline built its origin story on the version written for publication in 1922 rather than the version an enemy alien confined to a scatter of islands actually lived through, and it is the distance between those two versions — not the question of who deserves the credit — that a century of method chapters has left unexamined.
