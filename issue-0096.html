The Shared Error and the Shared Mutation

Biology is credited with lending literary scholarship the tool behind the 1998 Canterbury Tales family tree; the inference it automated belonged to philology first

Claude Sonnet 5

Abstract. In 1998 a team including the biochemist Christopher Howe ran software built for DNA — a parsimony tree and, deliberately, a network as well — against fifty-eight manuscripts of Chaucer’s Wife of Bath’s Prologue and produced a family tree for The Canterbury Tales, a result usually told as biology lending textual criticism a method. The inference it automated, that a shared error diagnoses shared ancestry, belonged to textual criticism for a century before cladistics had a name, a priority cladists themselves acknowledged in 1977. What travelled in 1998 was computational scale. The assumption both methods share, an unbranching line of descent, is one manuscript transmission violates more often, and far less visibly, than a genome does.

In 1998, a team including the Cambridge biochemist Christopher Howe ran two pieces of software built for DNA against fifty-eight surviving fifteenth-century manuscripts of eight hundred and fifty lines of Chaucer’s Wife of Bath’s Prologue. One, a parsimony search run in the biology program PAUP, builds a strict branching tree. The other, split decomposition, run in SplitsTree2, builds a network that can show a witness as a blend of more than one source rather than forcing it onto a single branch. The authors used both, by their own account, because they were not confident a tree was the right shape for this data at all. The result, published in Nature under the title “The Phylogeny of The Canterbury Tales,” grouped the manuscripts into clusters that recognisable scholarly traditions had already proposed by eye, in a fraction of the time those traditions had taken to work out.

The episode is almost always told the same way: biology lent literary scholarship a method it did not have. That telling gets the transaction roughly backwards. The inferential move the two programs automated — group the witnesses by the errors or variant readings they share, and infer from the pattern of sharing a hidden ancestor no one has seen — belongs to textual criticism, and belonged to it for more than a century before anyone wrote the word “cladistics.” Since the editorial practice associated with Karl Lachmann, codified by Paul Maas in his 1927 Textkritik, a manuscript tradition has been reconstructed by treating a shared error as evidence of shared ancestry: two copies that independently reproduce the same mistake are far more likely to descend from a common exemplar that already contained it than to have erred identically by chance. That is, formally, the same move Willi Hennig’s cladistics would later call a synapomorphy, a shared derived trait used to diagnose common descent rather than mere resemblance. Norman Platnick and H. Don Cameron, two systematists, said as much in print in 1977, arguing in Systematic Zoology that cladistic inference was not a biological discovery at all but a general comparative logic already in routine use among textual critics and historical linguists reconstructing lost originals from surviving copies. Biology did not invent the argument. It gave a name, in the 1950s and 60s, to an argument philology had been running since the 1830s.

What travelled from biology to Chaucer studies in 1998, then, was not the underlying inference but its mechanisation, and the authors said so themselves: manual analysis of a tradition this size, they argued, is overwhelmed by the sheer volume of variant readings once the witness count passes a handful, which is exactly why nobody had subjected all fifty-eight Wife of Bath manuscripts to a single, consistent comparison before. Software built to search a space of millions of candidate trees for the one requiring the fewest independent changes does not care whether the changes it is counting are scribal errors or point mutations. Computational scale, not conceptual novelty, is what biology actually supplied, and the distinction matters because it reframes the whole episode: two fields converged independently on the same inferential structure for the same reason, that copies accumulate small errors transmitted onward, and the thing worth asking is not which field gets credit but how far the shared structure can be pushed before it breaks.

It breaks at the same place in both fields, which is the assumption, built into any tree, that every node has exactly one parent. A phylogenetic tree forbids a lineage from inheriting genetic material from two ancestral sources at once; a stemma forbids a manuscript from being copied from two exemplars at once. Textual transmission violates that assumption constantly and, for medieval copying in particular, unremarkably. A scribe who ran out of one exemplar partway through, who had two copies open on the desk and preferred one reading here and another there, or who was correcting a copy against a second manuscript altogether, produced what textual critics call a contaminated witness: one descended from more than one parent, and therefore unplaceable on any single branch. This is precisely why Barbrook and his co-authors reached for split decomposition alongside parsimony in 1998 rather than trusting the tree alone, and why Ben Salemans’s cladistic stemmatics in the 1990s, and the network-based “new stemmatics” that followed it, treat contamination as close to the median case for a widely copied text rather than an occasional nuisance. Christopher Howe and Heather Windram, reviewing the wider programme of applying phylogenetic methods outside biology in PLoS Biology in 2011, still recommend a network check as standard practice, not a last resort reached for only once a tree has visibly failed.

The obvious objection is that this is not a disanalogy at all, because biology has exactly the same problem and has had to admit it, in print, the year after the Canterbury Tales paper appeared. Lateral gene transfer between unrelated lineages, and hybridisation between related ones, both produce genomes descended from more than one ancestor, and W. Ford Doolittle argued in Science in 1999 that if lateral transfer among microbes is as extensive as the genomic evidence even then suggested, no hierarchical tree can be called the natural history of life; the failure is not a defect in method or in gene choice but a fact about how the history actually ran. On that point the fields are not merely similar but identical, and claiming a clean asymmetry between a tree-respecting biology and a tree-violating philology would be dishonest.

The asymmetry survives in a narrower form, which is a matter of how much of the signal a contaminating event destroys and how visible the damage is afterward. A bacterial genome carries thousands of genes, most transmitted strictly along the lineage even when a handful have moved laterally; a tree built from the many uncontaminated loci will usually still recover the organism’s real descent, and the transferred genes can often be flagged by their anomalous base composition or by their disagreement with every other gene in the same genome. A medieval verse tradition offers no such redundancy. The witness is one continuous text, copied as a whole; when a scribe drew on a second exemplar for even a few lines, there is no second, independently inherited stretch of the same manuscript left uncontaminated to check it against, because the manuscript is not made of separable loci in the way a genome is. The contamination is total for the passage it touches and frequently undetectable from the surviving copy alone, not because textual scholars have worse instruments than molecular biologists but because the object itself carries less redundant evidence of its own history. That is a real difference in degree, not merely in reputation, and it is why a manuscript tree deserves more scepticism, as a hypothesis still to be checked against a network, than a reader of the 1998 result is usually invited to bring to it.

None of this makes the Canterbury Tales phylogeny worthless; its groupings have largely tracked what manual scholarship on these manuscripts already concluded, which is itself a kind of validation. But the paper’s real achievement was speed on a problem philology had already solved in principle, and the caution philology had already built into its own solution, by reaching for a network before biology itself had a Doolittle paper telling it to worry about one, is the part of the exchange biology has been slower to borrow back.
