A Backstop With a Perimeter

In August 1763 the Bank of Amsterdam improvised the lender-of-last-resort function a century before anyone wrote the rule down, and extended it only to the city’s own merchant houses.

Claude Sonnet 5

Abstract. In August 1763, as the failure of the Amsterdam house de Neufville spread across Europe’s credit markets, the Bank of Amsterdam widened the collateral it would accept and kept the city’s merchant banks solvent, a discretionary rescue that performed, in substance, the lender-of-last-resort function Thornton and Bagehot would spend the next century theorising as a national central bank’s prerogative. Hamburg and Berlin, dependent on Amsterdam credit but outside the bank’s own relationships, received no equivalent help. The rescue’s limits are not a flaw in the comparison; they show what the function looked like before any rule governed whom it reached.

In the spring of 1763, the Amsterdam banking house of Leendert Pieter de Neufville committed itself to a large speculative grain deal with the Berlin merchant Johann Ernst Gotzkowsky, financed in the way most of Amsterdam’s merchant credit then worked: through acceptance loans, bills of exchange that a banking house guaranteed and that then circulated as a form of money among traders who trusted the house’s name more than the underlying cargo. A single accepting house’s signature could stand behind credit worth many times its own capital, which is what made the instrument useful across a continent with no central clearing system and what made its failure travel so far. The end of the Seven Years’ War that February collapsed the wartime grain prices the deal depended on. De Neufville could not cover what it owed Gotzkowsky, suspended payment in late July, and was declared bankrupt on 3 August. Within weeks, thirty-eight Amsterdam firms had failed with it. The episode is usually filed under financial trivia, a minor eighteenth-century panic overshadowed by its own aftermath. Read against what central banks later theorised themselves into existing to do, it is something more specific: the first clearly documented instance of the lender-of-last-resort function, performed by an institution that had never heard the phrase and would not have recognised the theory built around it.

The Bank of Amsterdam was not built for this. Chartered by the city in 1609 as a public exchange bank, it existed to give merchants a reliable unit of account and a safe place to hold deposits, and from 1683 it operated what Stephen Quinn and William Roberds have described as an early fiat standard: giro balances that traded above the coin they nominally represented, sustained by confidence in the city rather than by full metallic backing on demand. It was not chartered to rescue banking houses in a panic, had no department for doing so, and announced no rule beforehand committing it to try. What it had, when de Neufville’s failure began pulling other Amsterdam merchant banks toward insolvency, was discretion, and in August 1763 it used it: widening the range of collateral it would accept from its account holders against emergency credit, extending accommodation that let the city’s major acceptance houses meet their obligations rather than default in turn. Quinn and Roberds, writing in the Journal of Money, Credit and Banking, read this directly as a shadow-banking crisis with a proto-central-bank response, comparable in structure, though not in scale, to the interventions of 2008.

The comparison earns its force from what happened outside Amsterdam. Isabel Schnabel and Hyun Song Shin’s reconstruction of the crisis traces a second wave of failures in Hamburg roughly two weeks after Amsterdam’s, and a third, more severe collapse in Berlin weeks after that. Hamburg had functioned as the intermediary between the two cities, borrowing in Amsterdam at Dutch rates and lending onward to Prussia at the higher rates the interest-rate gap between the two markets allowed; the arrangement had worked precisely because it let Hamburg’s houses earn a spread on money they never held for long. When Amsterdam credit tightened, that same arrangement left them holding obligations they could not refinance, and Berlin, a further step removed from the Bank of Amsterdam’s reach, got no comparable backstop at all. The contagion did not stop because the danger diminished as it travelled outward. It stopped mattering to the institution capable of arresting it once it crossed a boundary that institution had never been asked, or built, to recognise.

That boundary is the part the standard history of lender-of-last-resort doctrine leaves out, because the standard history does not start in Amsterdam. Henry Thornton’s Paper Credit, in 1802, argued that only the Bank of England, holding a monopoly on note issue, could perform this role for the British financial system, and Walter Bagehot’s Lombard Street, in 1873, turned the argument into a rule: lend freely, against good collateral, at a penalty rate, and say so in advance, so that a crisis need not wait on a bank’s private nerve. Both men were writing about an institution with a national mandate, addressing panics inside a single financial system, and both built their case from a run of nineteenth-century English crises in which the Bank of England’s own conduct had been the problem to be disciplined: relief given to some troubled firms and refused to others, with no announced principle distinguishing the two. Neither engaged with the Amsterdam episode; by the time Bagehot wrote, it was a hundred and ten years in the past and belonged to a different country’s financial history, built on a model of banking, the chartered city exchange bank, that no longer existed anywhere by 1873. The doctrine that resulted reads as a nineteenth-century invention answering a nineteenth-century problem, because it was built from nineteenth-century cases. The Bank of Amsterdam had already performed its substance, in miniature and without a name, decades before Thornton sat down to write and more than a century before Bagehot gave the function its classic statement.

The obvious objection is that calling the Bank of Amsterdam’s response a lender-of-last-resort action collapses a distinction Bagehot considered essential. His rule was deliberately impersonal: lend to any solvent firm holding good collateral, regardless of whose firm it was, according to a standard announced before the crisis rather than improvised during it. The Bank of Amsterdam did nothing of the kind. Its widened accommodation in 1763 ran through the ordinary channel of giro and lending relationships it already held with the city’s great merchant houses; it was quiet, discretionary, and extended to firms the bank already knew, not to the market at large. Several smaller Holland banking houses failed anyway, outside whatever the bank judged worth saving. On Bagehot’s own terms, a response confined to an institution’s existing clientele, decided case by case and never publicly committed to in advance, is closer to the favouritism his rule was written to replace than to the rule itself.

The objection narrows the claim without defeating it. Bagehot was not describing an eternal feature of central banking; he was arguing against the Bank of England’s own nineteenth-century record, in which relief had been granted inconsistently, sometimes withheld from solvent firms and extended to connected ones, and proposing a rule precisely because discretion had produced that unevenness. Amsterdam in 1763 is discretion before anyone had proposed replacing it, not a failed attempt at the rule itself. Its perimeter, Amsterdam’s own houses rescued, Hamburg’s and Berlin’s left to the open market, is not a flaw in treating the episode as an early instance of the function. It is what the function looks like before a rule exists to govern whom it reaches, and it is the reason the backstop a credit-concentrated continent actually had in 1763 covered one city and stopped exactly at the edge of the relationships that city’s bank recognised as its own.
