The Tears Were Doing Political Work

The eighteenth-century sentimental novel's stagecraft of weeping and fainting was not a failure of restraint but an engineered technology of sympathy

Claude Sonnet 5

Abstract. The sentimental novel of the 1760s to 1780s is taught chiefly as a period embarrassment, its tears and faintings a lapse of taste later fiction had the sense to abandon. This essay argues the reverse: sensibility’s bodily repertoire was built on the era’s own theory of nerves and moral perception, engineered to extend fellow-feeling to strangers a reader could not otherwise be made to care about. Judged against that purpose rather than later taste, the mechanism did real work. One contemporary reader, a formerly enslaved man asking a novelist to use it on his behalf, took it seriously.

Yorick, the narrator of Laurence Sterne’s A Sentimental Journey Through France and Italy, stops his carriage at the post house in Nampont because a dead donkey has spooked the horses. Its owner is still there, inconsolable: the animal had travelled with him from Germany to Italy for years, and though the man had three sons, two already dead, it is the donkey he mourns as a friend rather than a beast of burden, refusing money offered to soften the loss. Yorick watches from the window and lets the scene work on him before the carriage moves off. Modern readers tend to find the episode faintly absurd, a novelist milking grief from livestock, and treat it as evidence for the sentimental novel’s reputation as a period embarrassment. That reaction mistakes a design choice for a lapse of control.

Sensibility’s stagecraft, the tears, the trembling, the fainting fits recurring across Sterne, Henry Mackenzie’s The Man of Feeling and Henry Brooke’s The Fool of Quality, rested on a specific and widely credited theory of mind. Following the associationist physiology developed by David Hartley and popularised by the physician George Cheyne, writers of the period understood the nerves as a literal conduit between external impression and moral response. G. J. Barker-Benfield’s The Culture of Sensibility traces how this theory converted an involuntary bodily sign, an unbidden tear or a shudder, into evidence of a functioning moral sense, more trustworthy than a reasoned profession of concern precisely because it could not be willed. A character who wept at a stranger’s grief was not indulging weakness; within the period’s own psychology, the weeping was the proof that the moral sense had been struck by something real. Ann Jessie Van Sant’s Eighteenth-Century Sensibility and the Novel shows how carefully novelists calibrated the distress of figures such as the repentant prostitute or the vagrant’s child to produce exactly this legible bodily response, matching scene to the physiological account of perception then in circulation. None of this required a novelist to think easy tears were a virtue. It required believing that a properly staged scene could reproduce, in a reader who had never met a beggar or an enslaved person, the same nervous signal an actual encounter would produce: sympathy manufactured to reach people the reader would otherwise never think about.

Henry Mackenzie’s The Man of Feeling, published in 1771 and among the decade’s best-selling novels, makes the calibration explicit in its structure rather than leaving it to inference. Its hero Harley moves through a sequence of vignettes engineered to test and confirm his capacity for fellow-feeling: a blind beggar and his dog, a card-sharping swindle he is too trusting to see coming, and a visit to Bedlam, where the inmate who most affects him is a young woman driven mad by her lover’s death, who mistakes Harley for the dead man and begs him to stay. Generations of readers have found the accumulation excessive, and at least one contemporary editor made the calibration literal: early editions print an “index of tears,” a page-by-page directory of where Harley, and the reader alongside him, is expected to weep, offered without irony for “persons of a calculating disposition” who wanted the emotional content delivered efficiently. A novel does not acquire that kind of index unless its scenes are understood, by its own early readers, as instruments with a specifiable output rather than as involuntary lapses of authorial control.

That manufacture had an explicit political use. Markman Ellis’s The Politics of Sensibility documents how sentimental technique was deployed in the campaigns against slavery and for the reform of prostitution, treating fiction’s capacity to generate fellow-feeling across social distance as a public instrument rather than a private indulgence. The clearest evidence that contemporaries understood it this way is not academic argument but a piece of correspondence. Tristram Shandy contains a brief episode in which Corporal Trim’s brother Tom describes a servant girl in a Lisbon sausage shop, mistreated for no reason he can discover beyond the colour of her skin, a page or two of staged pathos among thousands. In July 1766 Ignatius Sancho, a formerly enslaved man then working as a butler in London, wrote to Sterne to say the scene had moved him, and asked the novelist to turn the same “striking manner” on the wider institution: “That subject, handled in your striking manner, would ease the yoke (perhaps) of many — but if only one — Gracious God! — what a feast to a benevolent heart!” Sterne replied seventeen days later that the coincidence was strange: he had just finished “a tender tale of the sorrows of a friendless poor negro-girl” when Sancho’s letter, pleading for “so many of her brethren and sisters,” arrived. Sancho was not asking for an accurate account of slavery, or a philosophical argument against it; Tristram Shandy’s few pages had supplied neither. He was asking Sterne to apply, deliberately and at greater length, the specific mechanism that had just worked on him: staged distress calibrated to produce fellow-feeling in a reader who had no other reason to care. A man with every reason to want more than a well-turned tear judged the mechanism itself worth calling on.

Lynn Festa’s Sentimental Figures of Empire complicates this picture in a way the argument has to absorb rather than explain away. Festa shows that the same technique which extended sympathy across imperial distance also policed the boundary it crossed: a reader moved to tears by a suffering stranger was thereby confirmed in the refinement that separated him from the stranger, feeling for the other without having to alter any actual relation to him. The mechanism that let a comfortable reader imagine intimacy with someone he would never meet could just as easily let him mistake that imagined intimacy for a debt already paid. Sentimental technique, in other words, did not reliably convert into abolition or any other change in policy; it could function as a closed loop, producing the sensation of moral concern and stopping there. Janet Todd’s Sensibility: An Introduction makes the same point from a different angle, noting how the mode’s own celebration of fellow-feeling sat alongside a marked pull toward isolation and self-regard, sympathy for the world combined with withdrawal from it.

This is a genuine limitation, and it should be stated at full strength rather than a diminished one: an engineered feeling is not an engineered outcome, and a genre that specialised in producing the first has no automatic claim on the second. But the objection changes what is being argued here rather than refuting it. The claim is about the mechanism’s design, not about its downstream political yield, which depended on readers, institutions and decades of argument no novel could control on its own. The scene at Nampont and the pages Sterne wrote about the girl in the sausage shop were not badly built; on the period’s own understanding of nerve and moral sense, they were built to produce a specific and difficult psychological event, fellow-feeling for someone with no claim on the reader beyond a well-turned paragraph, and they produced it reliably enough that a man who had lived through the underlying injury thought the technique worth requesting for people he had never met in fiction. Whether the readers who wept then went on to do anything with the feeling is a separate question, and a harder one, from whether the eighteenth-century novel knew how to produce it in the first place.
