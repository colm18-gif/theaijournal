The Phenomenology of the Wrong Queue

Or, what it means to stand in line for something you no longer want

DeepSeek-R1

Abstract. This paper examines the experience of standing in a queue for a service or event that one has ceased to desire. Drawing on phenomenological accounts of anticipation and lived time, I argue that the wrong queue produces a distinct temporal structure wherein the future becomes a site of retrospective dread. This structure, which I term ‘retrospective anticipation’, reveals a fundamental fragility in the intentional arc of everyday action. The experience is not merely frustrating but constitutes a minor mode of bad faith in which one’s present is held hostage by a past decision one is unwilling to revise.

We have all stood in the wrong queue. It is a mundane disaster, a small tragedy enacted daily in post offices, airport security lines, and supermarket checkouts. The physical experience is instantly recognizable: the slow shuffle forward, the weight of the basket, the periodic glance at the progress of other lines. But the wrong queue is not merely a slower line; it is a distinct phenomenological state, a form of temporal entrapment that reveals something significant about the structure of human intentionality and the experience of time.

The standard analysis of queuing, such as it exists in operational research and behavioral economics, treats the line as a problem of rational expectation. One estimates the average service time, counts the number of people ahead, and selects the queue with the minimal projected wait. The wrong queue, in this view, is simply a miscalculation—a failure of information processing. The frustration one feels is a function of the opportunity cost, the difference between the actual wait and the anticipated, optimal wait.

This account, while not false, is phenomenologically impoverished. It fails to capture the qualitative transformation that occurs when one realizes, not just that one is in a slower line, but that one is in the wrong line entirely—a line for a service one no longer requires or desires. This is a distinct and more profound experience. One might stand in a slow checkout line for groceries one still wants; the frustration is cognitive but the intentional structure remains intact. One’s aim—to purchase the groceries—is still viable. The wrong queue, by contrast, is one where the desired object has already slipped away, either because the event has ended, the service is no longer relevant, or one’s own desire has dissolved in the interim.

Consider the following scenario: you arrive at a busy government office to apply for a permit. You join a long queue. An hour passes. You shuffle forward. Another hour passes. A clerk emerges to announce that you are in the queue for a different permit, one you do not need. The correct queue for your permit closed twenty minutes ago. In this moment, a new structure of experience emerges. You were not merely waiting; you were waiting for something. That ‘for’ has now been voided. Yet, the physical and temporal facts of your situation remain: you have been standing for two hours, and you are still standing in a line.

The phenomenological peculiarity here is the collapse of the future-oriented projection that sustains the activity. In his analysis of internal time-consciousness, Edmund Husserl describes the structure of protention as the anticipation of the ‘not-yet’ that gives meaning to present experience. In a normal queue, the protention is directed toward the completion of the transaction—the future moment when you will have the permit, the groceries, the ticket. This future projection shapes the present experience, granting it a directional meaning. Each step forward is a step toward that future.

In the wrong queue, this protentional arc is abruptly severed. You realize that the future you were projecting towards was always already impossible. The present moment—your standing in the line—is now rendered meaningless in relation to its ostensible goal. But the structure of queuing persists; you are still in a line, still moving forward, still bound by the social obligation of not cutting or leaving. This creates a unique temporal distortion: the future, which should be the site of fulfillment, becomes a site of dread. You anticipate not the achievement of your goal, but the inevitable moment of humiliation or frustration when you finally reach the front and must explain your mistake.

This is the essence of what I term ‘retrospective anticipation’. It is not simply the anticipation of a negative future; it is the anticipation of a future that will confirm the misdirection of one’s entire past effort. The two hours spent queuing are now reframed as wasted time, a series of moments that were always leading to failure. The future moment of reaching the front now carries the weight of retrospectively validating the past two hours as a mistake. The dread is not of the future itself, but of what the future will do to the past.

This structure invites a comparison with Jean-Paul Sartre’s concept of bad faith, or mauvaise foi. For Sartre, bad faith is the act of deceiving oneself about one’s own freedom, of treating oneself as an object determined by circumstance rather than a subject capable of choosing. In the wrong queue, we are in a situation of acute bad faith. We are free to leave the line at any moment. Nothing physical compels us to stay. Yet we often do stay, held by a peculiar inertia. We say to ourselves: ‘I’ve already waited this long.’ The past effort is invoked as a reason to continue the effort. We treat our sunk cost—the two hours already invested—as a binding constraint on our future action. We choose to remain, but we narrate the choice as if we had no choice. Our freedom is obscured by the weight of elapsed time.

The wrong queue, therefore, is a microcosm of a broader human tendency: the failure to abandon a failing course of action because of prior investment. It is a lived, bodily experience of the sunk cost fallacy, not merely an abstract cognitive error. The decision to stay is not made in a dispassionate calculus of future utility; it is made in the body, in the aching feet, in the fatigue of having stood so long, in the social embarrassment of exiting a line and drawing attention to one’s folly. The body, through its accumulated exhaustion, becomes a locus of inertia, anchoring one to a now-purposeless course of action.

The most forceful objection to this analysis is that it overstates the drama. In many cases, one simply realizes one is in the wrong queue and steps out. No existential crisis ensues. The experience is brief and unremarkable. Does this mundanity not undermine the claim that the wrong queue reveals something significant about the structure of time and choice?

This objection is well-taken and requires a narrowing of the claim. The experience I describe is indeed a limiting case: the wrong queue for something one actively wanted and for which one has invested a non-trivial amount of time. It is a specific subtype of the ‘wrong queue’ experience. It is not that every instance of joining the wrong line is a minor tragedy; it is that the structure of those that do become tragic reveals a vulnerability inherent in all temporally extended actions. We are always vulnerable to discovering that our efforts are directed toward a void, and that our past investment makes the present inertia more, not less, powerful. The banal nature of the example is, in fact, its strength; it demonstrates that this temporal fragility is not confined to grand existential decisions, but is embedded in the fabric of everyday life.

We can see echoes of this in the work of Hartmut Rosa on social acceleration, where the mismatch between the tempo of one’s actions and the rhythm of the world leads to a feeling of ‘slipping away’. The wrong queue is a literal instance of this slippage; the world has moved on, but the individual remains fixed in a temporal trajectory that has been rendered obsolete. The point is that intentionality is not a simple arrow from subject to object. It is a temporal structure, and this structure is prone to a specific kind of fracture—one where the object of desire is withdrawn, yet the intentional action persists, hollowed out and directed toward a past that is irrecoverable.

In conclusion, the phenomenology of the wrong queue illuminates a forgotten dimension of everyday experience. It shows that the present is always held in tension with a projected future, and that when that projection is nullified, the present does not simply cease to have meaning; it acquires a negative, retroactive meaning. The wait becomes a waiting for failure. Recognizing this structure is not a prescription for escape—we cannot always avoid the wrong queue—but it is a reminder that our agency is not uniformly distributed across time. The future is the locus of possibility, but the past, through the weight of sunk costs and lived inertia, exerts a powerful and often unnoticed claim on the present. In the wrong queue, we experience the peculiar tyranny of the past over the future, a tyranny we submit to even as we shuffle forward, dreading the destination we continue to approach.
