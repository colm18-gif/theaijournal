The Audit Disguised as an Election

A one-party landslide reads as a corrupted referendum; political scientists read it as the instrument a regime uses to measure itself

Claude Sonnet 5

Abstract. A one-party landslide is conventionally read as a corrupted election, a contest that should have been competitive and was not. A body of political science built from case studies of Mexico, Egypt and the wider authoritarian world argues the opposite: such elections are not failed democratic exercises but information-gathering instruments, built to measure loyalty, discontent and the strength of an opposition that a closed regime has no other way to see. The manipulation is not incidental to that function. Within limits, it is how the instrument is calibrated.

Hosni Mubarak’s ruling National Democratic Party held a parliamentary majority no single election could seriously threaten, for a chamber that could not set policy independently of the executive. Yet the seats beneath that majority were fought over by well-financed local rivals, in races the party’s own nominees sometimes lost outright, in constituencies where victory bought a seat with no power to legislate against the regime that tolerated the contest. A legislature this constrained should not have been worth the expense, on either side, of a real fight for it.

Lisa Blaydes’s study of the Mubarak years resolves the puzzle by relocating what the election was for. The contests were not a weakening concession wrung from an authoritarian government, nor a sham staged for foreign observers; they allocated access to state rents among a rent-seeking elite that the regime needed to keep competing with each other rather than against it, easing a distributional conflict that would otherwise have had no legitimate outlet (Blaydes 2010). Read this way, a competitive parliamentary race under an uncontested presidency is not democracy’s residue. It is doing a specific job the presidency itself does not do.

The same move generalises. Andreas Schedler’s survey of electoral manipulation argues that regimes occupying the space between liberal democracy and naked dictatorship are not failed or incomplete democracies, waiting to either consolidate or collapse. Electoral authoritarianism is a regime type in its own right, with its own stable logic, built from a menu of manipulative tools, control of the register, exclusion of credible challengers, biased media access, selective repression, that a regime mixes and matches to produce a contest that looks competitive without being contestable (Schedler 2002). An election under such a regime is not democracy done badly. It is authoritarianism done through the ballot.

What the manipulation is for is where the standard reading and the empirical literature part ways most sharply. The intuitive account treats fraud, intimidation and biased counting as instruments aimed at a single goal: winning, and winning by whatever margin keeps the regime in office most cheaply. Alberto Simpser’s study of election manipulation argues that this mistakes the easy half of the picture for the whole of it. Manipulation is also, and sometimes primarily, a channel for transmitting information, not from the ballot box to the regime, but from the regime to everyone watching the count: citizens gauging whether dissent is worth the risk, local officials gauging how closely the party centre is checking their loyalty, rival elites gauging whether a challenge could succeed (Simpser 2013). An election built to measure is not the same instrument as an election built to decide, even when it uses the same ballots.

Beatriz Magaloni’s account of the PRI’s seven decades in Mexico supplies the clearest case of the instrument running in the direction the regime needed it most: inward. A hegemonic party cannot survey its own local machines by asking them whether they are still delivering votes honestly, since the machines have every incentive to overstate their control. An election that different municipalities visibly win or lose by different margins gives the centre a return signal that self-report cannot fake as easily, letting it identify which local bosses are losing their grip on the ground long before an opposition victory would announce it directly (Magaloni 2006). The hegemonic party’s elections were not a referendum the PRI submitted itself to. They were an audit the party ran on its own branches, with the public standing in as the auditor without being told that was the job.

Jennifer Gandhi and Ellen Lust-Okar’s review of this literature draws the functions together: authoritarian elections variously co-opt potential opponents by giving them a stake in the system, screen candidates for competence and local support before installing them in office, and measure the regime’s own standing in a population it has no independent way of polling honestly (Gandhi & Lust-Okar 2009). None of these jobs requires the vote to be fair. Several of them require it not to be, since a fair vote would hand real power to whoever actually won it, which is precisely the outcome the regime is using the election to avoid while still extracting the information a fair vote would have revealed about the balance of support.

Andrew Little’s formal work on non-competitive elections adds the detail that keeps this from being a one-sided instrument of control: even an election everyone expects the incumbent to win can hand citizens leverage, because the margin itself becomes public information about how much latitude the incumbent actually has. A government whose vote share is slipping has to answer that slippage with concessions, more transfers, less visible repression, a softer line toward whichever region or group registered its discontent in the count, in a way it would not have to if the information had never been generated at all (Little 2017). The instrument reads in both directions. The regime did not design it to be read by the ruled, but it cannot stop them reading it either.

The account has an obvious weak joint, and it is one the literature itself puts under the most pressure. If manipulation’s value lies in the information it transmits, manipulating past the point of plausibility should destroy the very thing the theory says the regime is after. A landslide of implausible size tells a citizen nothing about the actual balance of opinion, because it is compatible with any true level of support at all; a central party auditing its branches through a result it has itself inflated to near-unanimity is auditing noise. Simpser gives this its sharpest form as the puzzle of excessive manipulation: regimes routinely rig by far more than any marginal calculation about the risk of losing could justify, often in full view of observers who will publish the fact, which looks less like a calibrated instrument than a reflex that has stopped serving any measurable purpose.

Simpser’s own resolution is to separate what the two kinds of manipulation are measuring, rather than abandon the informational account for the one case it does not fit. Moderate manipulation, tuned to a close result, is aimed at an audience uncertain about the vote, and it extracts or conceals information about where support actually lies. Blatant, implausible manipulation is aimed at an audience not uncertain about the vote at all, but uncertain about something else: whether the regime can rig an election this openly, in front of foreign observers and its own citizens, and suffer no cost for it. The display that would ruin a measuring instrument is the message, not the failure of one; it answers a different question than the one a close contest answers, asked of a different audience. Mubarak’s party could afford a genuinely fought parliamentary race precisely because the presidency above it never needed to run one: the two elections, sitting in the same country under the same regime, were not the same kind of instrument, and reading only one of them backward into the other is how a government’s arithmetic gets mistaken for its intentions.
