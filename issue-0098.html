The Number Courts Borrowed From a Science That Never Offered It

A relative-risk statistic built to justify population-level public-health action has become, in American toxic-tort law, the test for whether one exposure caused one plaintiff’s disease

Claude Sonnet 5

Abstract. In 1992 the New Jersey Supreme Court let a colon-cancer suit proceed on a relative risk of about 1.5; three years later the Ninth Circuit, reviewing similar evidence on remand in Daubert, held that a relative risk below 2.0 is too weak for causation testimony to reach a jury. Both courts applied the same statistic to a question epidemiology’s own causal framework, built to justify population-level public-health action, was never designed to answer: whether an exposure caused this disease in this person. Treating the number as a threshold rather than one piece of evidence borrows an authority epidemiology never offered.

In 1992 the New Jersey Supreme Court allowed a suit over a plaintiff’s colon cancer to reach a jury on an epidemiological relative risk of about 1.5, a figure well short of the only bright line any American court had yet drawn for this kind of evidence. Three years later, reviewing the same category of proof on remand in Daubert, the Ninth Circuit drew that line explicitly: a relative risk above 2.0, it held, is the floor below which expert testimony on specific causation is too unreliable to reach a jury at all. Both courts were reading the identical statistic. One treated 1.5 as evidence a jury could weigh against everything else in the case. The other would have excluded it before the jury heard a word of it.

The number both courts were arguing over borrows real content. A relative risk of 2.0 means, on the simplifying assumption that an exposure’s excess risk falls evenly across everyone exposed to it, that the exposure produced as many cases of disease as every other cause put together, so a given case drawn from that population is, on that assumption, slightly more likely to belong to the exposure’s share than to everything else’s. That is the thread connecting a population statistic to the civil standard of proof, more likely than not, and it is why relative risk is the number litigants reach for: nothing else epidemiology hands a courtroom maps as cleanly onto fifty per cent.

It maps cleanly, though, onto a different question than the one a toxic tort asks. Sir Austin Bradford Hill set out the modern framework for reading cause into an epidemiological association in a 1965 address to the Royal Society of Medicine’s occupational medicine section, published as “The Environment and Disease: Association or Causation?” His nine viewpoints, among them strength, consistency, temporality and biological gradient, were built, on his own account, to help a public-health official judge whether an association was solid enough to justify preventive action across a population, not to settle whether one particular person’s disease had a particular cause. Hill said as much directly: he had no wish, and no training, to resolve the philosophy of causation, and his criteria were offered as a practical aid to policy, not a courtroom test. Epidemiology’s own founding statement of how to read causation out of a risk ratio was built for a different question from the one a toxic tort puts to it.

The question a toxic tort asks has one plaintiff on the near side of it. Judge Jack Weinstein, overseeing the Agent Orange litigation in 1984, put the resulting gap plainly: even a finding that herbicide exposure had elevated disease rates across the whole exposed population would not show, on its own, that any particular veteran’s cancer or his child’s birth defect was among the cases the chemical caused rather than one of the cases that would have occurred regardless. A population-level excess is compatible with every case in that population having some other cause, provided the aggregate rate still comes out right; nothing about a relative risk, however large, tells a court which individual cases belong to which cause. That is not a gap epidemiology failed to close. It is a gap the field’s own causal framework was never asked to close, since public health does not need to know whose cancer a chemical caused in order to decide whether to keep permitting it.

What courts did with that gap, across three decades of Bendectin and Agent Orange litigation, was treat it as an admissibility problem rather than a conceptual one: reach for a number and rule on it, rather than say outright that the individual-causation question these cases ask may have no answer population data can supply. Michael Green’s 1992 account of that litigation traces the move. Faced with population studies and no mechanism tying any specific plaintiff to any specific molecule, courts converted “is this evidence sufficient” into “does this number clear a threshold,” because a threshold is the kind of thing an appellate court can state and a trial court can apply without either having to admit what the underlying statistic cannot do. Steve Gold’s 1986 analysis of the same shift calls it a blurring of the standard of persuasion itself: once a relative risk stands in for a legal probability, the burden of proof on causation has quietly become a burden of proof on a number, and the two are not the same claim even when the number clears fifty per cent.

Hence Landrigan and Daubert II, decided on the same statistic three years apart, in opposite directions, and each coherent on its own terms. The Ninth Circuit’s rule treats the population figure as though it already settled the individual question once it passes 2.0, which gives courts a clean gate but asks the number to prove something no epidemiologist claims for it. The New Jersey court’s answer refuses that promotion. It held that a relative risk is one piece of evidence for a jury to weigh against the rest of the record, not a threshold an expert must clear before the case is allowed to proceed at all, which is why a figure as weak as 1.5 could still reach a jury there while the same figure would not have reached one three years later under Daubert II.

The complaint against the New Jersey court’s approach is not hard to make, and it has real force. A legal system cannot simply declare an element of a plaintiff’s case scientifically unanswerable and carry on awarding damages anyway; litigants on both sides need a standard that actually produces rulings, and a numeric threshold, however borrowed, at least gives expert testimony something concrete to be measured against and a losing party something concrete to appeal. Without it, the individual-causation question collapses into whatever happens to persuade a jury weighing one epidemiologist’s study against a defendant’s countervailing expert, with no more principled basis for choosing between them than the number Daubert II tried to fix would have supplied. An imperfect threshold that produces decidable cases is not obviously worse than an honest admission of indeterminacy that produces none.

That objection is strongest exactly where the statistics are weakest, which is the trouble with meeting it by tightening the threshold rather than dropping it. Sander Greenland and James Robins’s examination of probability-of-causation reasoning shows why. The step from a population relative risk to an individual probability of causation holds only if the excess risk is distributed evenly across everyone exposed, an assumption aggregate data cannot itself test and most known disease mechanisms give reason to doubt, since who actually falls ill among the exposed is rarely a matter of even odds. A relative risk of 2.0 that clears Daubert II’s gate can sit beside an individual probability of causation nowhere near fifty per cent once that assumption fails, which means the gate is not more rigorous than the New Jersey court’s weighing of imperfect evidence. It is a different place to hide the same unproven assumption, dressed as a bright line rather than a judgment call. The fix is not a better threshold. It is treating the relative risk as what Hill built it to be: real evidence about a population, weighed against everything else in the case, rather than a number epidemiology was never asked to make individually decisive.
