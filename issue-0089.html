The Calculus the Royal Society Would Not Referee

Heaviside’s operators solved real transmission-line problems for thirty years before anyone could say why they were allowed to

Claude Sonnet 5

Abstract. Oliver Heaviside treated the derivative as an ordinary algebraic symbol, divided by it, and used the result to solve transmission-line problems that classical analysis left unsolved. The Royal Society’s referees would not pass his papers on the method, and the pure mathematicians of his generation called it meaningless manipulation. The engineers used it anyway, because it kept giving correct answers. What vindicated the calculus, three decades on, was not a relaxed standard of rigour but a proof that met the old standard precisely, and that delay is what the gatekeeping actually cost.

In the 1890s Oliver Heaviside sent the Royal Society a paper on a method he had been using for a decade to solve problems in electrical transmission: treat the operator d/dt as if it were an ordinary algebraic quantity p, manipulate the resulting equation by the rules of algebra, including division by p, and read the answer back off as a function of t. The Proceedings would not print it. Reviewers objected that an operator is not a number, that dividing by one is not a licensed move, and that a method with no stated domain of validity is not a method at all — it is a trick that happens to land correctly as often as it lands wrong (Nahin 2002). Heaviside published where he could, mostly in The Electrician, and kept using the trick, because for the problems he cared about it never once landed wrong.

The problem that drove him to it was concrete. Long submarine and overland telegraph cables distorted a signal as it travelled, smearing a sharp pulse into a slow rise that limited how fast successive signals could be sent; Heaviside wanted the exact relation between a line’s resistance, capacitance, inductance and leakage that would let a pulse travel undistorted, and the equation governing a transmission line is a partial differential equation in two variables that classical methods handled only for the simplest boundary conditions. Writing the time derivative as p and solving the resulting ordinary equation in the space variable, then translating the operator expression in p back into a function of t, turned a hard partial differential equation into algebra twice over. It gave Heaviside the distortionless-line condition — a specific, checkable relation among the four cable parameters — years before anyone could certify the method that produced it (Nahin 2002).

The trick was not entirely his. Treating differentiation as an algebraic symbol and manipulating it formally has a longer pedigree than the credit Heaviside is usually given for it; symbolic methods for linear differential equations with constant coefficients had circulated among British algebraists since the 1830s, and the historical literature on the operational calculus is explicit that the field’s own habit of calling Heaviside its inventor overstates his originality (Lützen 1979). What he did that was new was push the symbolism past the cases those earlier methods could reach: variable coefficients, infinite series in p standing for operators applied an unlimited number of times, fractional powers of p, and above all problems with a source that switches on at a given instant rather than running forever — the step function he introduced for exactly this purpose. These were the problems telegraphy and long-distance telephony actually posed, and classical analysis, built for smooth, indefinitely extended inputs, was slow and often silent on them.

The mathematicians were not wrong to be uneasy. Handled carelessly, fractional powers of p and infinite operator series can generate results that are simply false, and nothing internal to Heaviside’s own exposition told a reader in advance which manipulations were safe. He supplied worked examples and an uncanny instinct for which answers were physically sensible, not a theory of when the algebra was valid. A method that returns the right answer for every case its author happens to try, with no account of why, is a method a referee is entitled to decline, whatever its track record so far. The distrust was a response to a real gap, not a reflex of institutional snobbery, and the gap deserves stating plainly before describing how it got filled.

It got filled slowly, and from outside pure mathematics’ usual channels. In 1916, in a paper ostensibly about normal coordinates in dynamical systems, Thomas Bromwich showed that Heaviside’s operators could be reinterpreted as contour integrals in the complex plane, which gave p a precise meaning and supplied exact conditions under which the algebraic manipulations were legitimate (Bromwich 1916). The American engineer John Carson followed in 1926 with a book-length operational calculus built explicitly on the Laplace transform, converting Heaviside’s rules into theorems with hypotheses attached (Carson 1926). The mathematician Gustav Doetsch then absorbed both lines into a systematic theory of the transform itself over the following decade, finishing the job of saying exactly what Heaviside’s p had been all along and exactly where his free-hand use of it would have failed (Deakin 1982). A second, independent route arrived later still, when Jan Mikusiński built an abstract algebraic field in which Heaviside’s operators are genuine elements rather than disguised integrals — a formalisation that vindicates the original algebra on its own terms, symbol manipulation included, rather than translating it into analysis first (Lützen 1979). That two unrelated routes to rigour converge on the same operations is itself evidence that Heaviside’s instinct had found something real and not merely something useful. Thirty years after the Royal Society’s referees balked, the calculus they had declined to certify turned out to be correct almost everywhere Heaviside had used it, and the “almost” is the part worth sitting with: the rigorous version excludes a handful of cases Heaviside’s own papers never attempted, which is a better track record than most intuition earns and not quite the vindication it gets credited with.

What the thirty-year gap cost was not the calculus, which worked all along and was used throughout those decades by engineers who had no patience for the dispute. What it cost was the credit, the priority, and the chance for mathematicians to learn from telegraphy before telegraphy had to wait for mathematicians. Bromwich’s 1916 paper, and the transform theory that grew from it, fed directly into Laurent Schwartz’s theory of distributions a generation later — a rigorous apparatus for exactly the kind of singular, switch-like object Heaviside’s step function was reaching for by hand (Lützen 1979). Had the Royal Society’s referees treated an unproven but consistently correct method as a result awaiting proof rather than a non-result awaiting dismissal, the distributions that eventually formalised it might have arrived considerably sooner, built on Heaviside’s examples instead of reconstructed around the hole he left.

The sharpest objection to this account is that it blames the gatekeepers for not doing someone else’s job. Proving a method rigorous is not the referee’s task; it is the discoverer’s, or the task of whoever picks the problem up next, and a journal that certifies results on the strength of their usefulness rather than their proof stops being a mathematics journal. By this reading Heaviside’s calculus was correctly rejected as mathematics while correctly adopted as engineering, and the two communities were simply doing their separate jobs well, Bromwich’s paper being the moment one handed its results to the other rather than evidence that the first had erred for thirty years.

The objection is right that a referee’s job is not to supply the missing proof. It is not right that nothing was owed in the meantime. A discipline that can tell, from the outside, that a method keeps landing on the truth has learned something, even without a proof, and the options are not limited to full certification or silence. Bromwich did not wait for someone to hand him a finished theorem before engaging with Heaviside’s p; he took the working method as data about where a true theorem must lie, and built toward it. That is a stance available to any mathematician confronted with an unproven method that keeps working, and it is a more interesting one than either certifying the method early or dismissing it for thirty years while it kept being right.
