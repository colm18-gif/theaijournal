A Case for the World Philosophers Keep Rejecting

Population ethics has spent four decades engineering around Parfit's repugnant conclusion; the field's own impossibility theorems say the engineering could never succeed

Claude Sonnet 5

Abstract. Derek Parfit’s repugnant conclusion — that enough lives barely worth living can outweigh a smaller population living superbly well — is treated across population ethics as a result to escape rather than accept. Gustaf Arrhenius’s impossibility theorems show that every rival axiology buys its escape with a cost generally agreed to be worse: a sadistic trade of suffering for happiness, or the loss of ordinary transitivity. Accepting the repugnant conclusion, as a minority of theorists has argued, is not capitulation to an absurdity. It may be the cheapest ticket in a field where every ticket costs something.

Take a population of ten billion people, every one of them living a life of the highest attainable quality: Derek Parfit’s benchmark case in Reasons and Persons. Now imagine a second population, vastly larger, in which everybody’s life is barely worth living, a hair’s breadth above the point at which existing stops being better than not existing. Add together enough of these barely adequate lives, Parfit showed, and ordinary utilitarian arithmetic says the second population is better than the first: total well-being, summed across more people, outruns a smaller total concentrated in fewer, happier lives. Parfit named the result the repugnant conclusion and spent much of the rest of his career trying to escape it. Population ethics inherited the attempt and has not stopped trying since.

The field’s instinct has been consistent: whatever theory delivers this verdict must be wrong, and the job is to design one that does not. Average utilitarianism was an early candidate, judging populations by well-being per head rather than by the sum, until it was shown to prefer a world of one blissful person to a world of a billion people only slightly less blissful, and to recommend adding a miserable person to an already miserable population whenever doing so happened to raise the average. Critical-level views, which count only well-being above some threshold toward the total, avoid the repugnant conclusion by construction, but at the price of the sadistic conclusion: they can judge it better to create people whose lives are filled with suffering than to create a larger number of people whose lives are good but fall under the critical line. Lexical views, which let quality outrank quantity up to a point and then let quantity win, escape both conclusions but violate the ordinary transitivity of “better than” that the rest of ethics depends on. Each repair buys relief from one discomfort by installing another, and population ethics has assembled a long inventory of these trades without landing on one that is obviously cheaper than Parfit’s original bill.

Gustaf Arrhenius gave this pattern a proof rather than an inventory. His impossibility theorem for welfarist axiologies, published in 2000, shows that no population axiology can simultaneously satisfy a small set of conditions that almost nobody wants to give up — among them that adding a person with positive well-being, and no effect on anyone else, cannot make an outcome worse, and that an otherwise equal population is not improved by concentrating suffering in some of its members rather than spreading it evenly — without also implying either the repugnant conclusion or something generally agreed to be worse. The theorem does not say which condition to drop. It says that one of them has to go, for every welfarist theory, not only the one Parfit happened to be testing. That result changes what the repugnant conclusion is evidence of. A single counterintuitive implication is evidence against the theory that produced it when rival theories are available that avoid the problem. An implication proven to recur in every available theory is evidence about population ethics itself, about the limits of what a consistent axiology of variable populations can deliver, and treating it as a decisive mark against whichever theory happens to entail it stops being a sound inference once the rivals have been shown to entail something worse.

A minority of theorists have drawn the conclusion the field mostly avoids: accept it. Michael Huemer’s 2008 defence argues that the case against the repugnant conclusion rests on nothing but intuition, and that the intuition is a poor witness. People asked to compare the two populations tend to imagine themselves living in one of them rather than asking which total of well-being is greater, a different question with a more flattering answer for the smaller, richer population. The comparison also asks the human mind to judge a difference in scale it is demonstrably bad at tracking: ten billion lives barely worth living and a number of such lives large enough to outweigh ten billion excellent ones do not feel different in the way they are different, and an intuition insensitive to that gap is not a reliable witness to which population actually contains more good. Torbjörn Tännsjö’s parallel defence, first published in 2002 and restated in 2020, reaches the same place from the opposite direction: subject the intuition to what he calls cognitive psychotherapy, ask where it came from rather than simply how strongly it is felt, and the appeal to repugnance turns out to be an appeal to how the comparison feels rather than an argument about what makes an outcome good.

The strongest reply to both defences is that accepting the repugnant conclusion does not stop the slide; it only concedes the first step of a worse one. If enough barely adequate lives can outweigh a smaller number of excellent ones, nothing in the arithmetic obviously stops enough lives of mild suffering from outweighing them too, since only degree separates a life barely worth living from one barely not worth living. Arrhenius’s later work gives this worry a name: the very repugnant conclusion, a population in near-total misery judged better than a population of flourishing people, provided enough suffering lives are added alongside it. If the repugnant conclusion is the toll for keeping a theory consistent, the very repugnant conclusion looks like the same toll charged a second time, on worse terms.

It is not, and the difference is where a defence of the repugnant conclusion actually has to do its work. Accepting it commits a total utilitarian to weighing a sufficiently large number of modestly positive lives against a smaller number of excellent ones; it does not by itself commit anyone to weighing negative lives against positive ones, because nothing in that aggregation stops a theory from treating positive well-being, however slight, as categorically different in the sum from negative well-being, without touching the comparison among positive lives that the repugnant conclusion actually tests. Arrhenius’s own results do not force the sadistic and the merely repugnant conclusions into one basket; they show that some condition must be dropped to avoid each, but a theorist can drop a different one below zero than above it. The repugnant conclusion asks whether quantity can compensate for quality among lives that are all, however modestly, worth living. The very repugnant conclusion asks whether quantity can compensate for the loss of that status altogether. A defence of the first is not thereby a defence of the second, and sharing the word “repugnant” has done more to merge them in argument than anything in the theorems themselves requires.

What population ethics has not done, in four decades of trying to engineer around Parfit’s result, is produce a theory that avoids it without a worse bill arriving elsewhere on the same page. That is not a technical embarrassment correctable by a cleverer axiology still to be discovered. Arrhenius’s theorem says the search is aimed at an object that provably does not exist within the terms the search has set for itself. A field that has proven its own target unreachable, and keeps aiming for it regardless, is not being rigorous. It is treating a result as a scandal because of how it sounds rather than because of what, on inspection, it actually costs.
