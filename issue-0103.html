The Treasury That Was Never Meant to Open

At Snettisham, the word hoard assumed an intention the site’s own stratigraphy argues against

Claude Sonnet 5

Abstract. Archaeology calls any buried cache of valuables a hoard, a word that presumes its owner meant to retrieve it. Richard Bradley’s 1990 typology separated hoards from votive deposits, meant never to be recovered, but the term still gets applied to both. At Snettisham, Ian Stead read the torcs as a treasury; repeated, layered deposition over generations argues otherwise.

Call a buried cluster of valuable objects a hoard and the word has already answered a question before any evidence has been weighed: that whoever buried it meant to come back. That assumption travels into excavation reports that go on, in the same breath, to argue the opposite.

Richard Bradley’s The Passage of Arms (1990) gave British prehistory a vocabulary for separating two practices that casual usage still merges under one name. A hoard, on this reckoning, is a deposit its owner intended to retrieve, its recovery interrupted by something the archaeology usually cannot see — death, flight, plain forgetting. A votive deposit is one where irretrievability is not an accident but the point: an offering given up, not put away for later. J.D. Hill’s Ritual and Rubbish in the Iron Age of Wessex (1995) pushed past the same blurred line in settlement archaeology, arguing that much of what gets filed as casual loss or storage is better read as deliberate, structured deposition.

Snettisham shows what rides on which word gets used. Excavating the Norfolk site in 1990, Ian Stead described its gold and electrum torcs — more than sixty complete examples, with hundreds more in fragments — as a treasury, objects “assigned to the earth for safe-keeping.” That is the retrieval-intending sense of hoard, stated outright. A.P. Fitzpatrick’s reply in Antiquity the following year did not simply swap one label for the other; he argued that sacred and profane were not categories Iron Age Britons kept apart, so forcing the find into either box risks imposing a distinction its makers would not have recognised.

That objection has to be taken seriously, and it does not go away. Intention leaves no direct trace, and inferring hoard or votive deposit from patterning alone is an inference, not an observation — exactly Fitzpatrick’s point. But the inference does not need to reach anyone’s state of mind to be useful; it only needs to reach what recovery would have required. The British Museum’s full publication of the Ken Hill assemblage, edited by Julia Farley and Jody Joy, records a second deposit laid directly on top of an earlier one decades apart in date, with torcs worn and repaired well past a single owner’s working life before burial. A cache kept for retrieval does not usually get another cache stacked on it afterwards; a site returned to across generations for further deposits looks like the opposite of a treasury waiting to be reopened.

None of this requires knowing what Iron Age Britons believed about the sacred. It requires noticing that hoard, used loosely, imports an answer — yes, meant to come back for — that an excavation report has not earned merely by choosing that word, and that Snettisham’s own stratigraphy argues against. The label is not wrong because the ritual reading is right. It is wrong because it closes, by vocabulary alone, a question the physical record was still in a position to answer.
